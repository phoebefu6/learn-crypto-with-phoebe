Benford's law on reported volume

A venue reports its own volume and nobody audits it. You cannot inspect its matching engine, but you can read the sizes it publishes, and the sizes carry a fingerprint. When many traders with many habits each pick a number, the first digits of those numbers settle into a known shape: about 30 percent start with a 1 and under 5 percent with a 9. One bot with one habit breaks the shape. Tonight is that law, the reason it holds, two ways to measure how far a tape sits from it, a line calibrated on honest venues rather than borrowed from a textbook, and the uncomfortable result that the most naive wash bot is the last thing this test sees.

Harrowgate cannot audit a venue, but it can read the venue's own sizes

Harrowgate is a constructed data team at a fund-administration firm. Session 3 left it with an index that weights venues by volume, and a nagging question underneath: volume according to whom? Each venue publishes its own tape and its own total, there is no auditor, and a venue that inflates its volume climbs every table that ranks by it. Harrowgate cannot see inside any venue. What it can do is run a test on the trade sizes the venue itself published, a test that needs nothing from the venue except the column it already sends. LiveA test that asks nothing of the venue3 min▶

Most data-quality checks compare a source against something else: a second feed, a ledger, a reconciliation. A self-reported volume figure has nothing else to compare against, because the venue is the only party that saw its own trades. A forensic test on the sizes is different in kind. It compares the tape against a mathematical expectation about how numbers chosen by many independent people are distributed, and the venue cannot opt out of it without changing the sizes it publishes.

That makes it a screen, not a verdict. It can say a tape's sizes look like the output of many habits or look like the output of one. It cannot say who traded, whether a trade was economic, or how much volume is real. Every result tonight is read with that boundary in view.

Real world

Harrowgate's venue table had one venue whose reported volume grew steadily while its quoted spread and its number of price levels did not change at all. Nobody on the team could say whether that was success or decoration. Before arguing about it, they ran the first-digit test on a day of that venue's prints next to a day from a venue they had reason to trust, and argued about the numbers instead. Harrowgate is constructed; the situation is common.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • First significant digit. The leftmost non-zero digit of a number. 0.223 units and 223,000 micro-units both have first digit 2.
  • Benford's law, the first-digit law. The prediction that the first digit d appears with probability log10(1 + 1/d), so 1 leads 30.1 percent of the time and 9 leads 4.6 percent.
  • Conform. A tape conforms when its observed first-digit shares sit close to the law's. "Close" is the whole argument of Part 2.
  • MAD. Mean absolute deviation: the average, over the nine digits, of the gap between observed share and expected share, ignoring sign.
  • Chi-square. A goodness-of-fit statistic on the nine counts, on 8 degrees of freedom. Unlike MAD it grows with the number of trades.
  • Calibration. Measuring the honest spread of a reading on tapes you trust before judging a tape you do not.
  • Wash share. The share of a venue's reported volume that is wash: matched buy and sell pairs of the same size, a few seconds apart, that move nothing. Session 6 plants them on purpose.
Part 1 · covers the first-digit law

Why one comes first thirty percent of the time

14 min live

The law is short: the first digit d appears with probability log10(1 + 1/d). For d from 1 to 9 that is 30.1, 17.6, 12.5, 9.7, 7.9, 6.7, 5.8, 5.1 and 4.6 percent. The surprise is not the formula; it is that real collections of numbers obey it, and the explanation for why they do is exactly the explanation for why a wash bot does not.

LiveThe law, and where it came from5 min▶

The rule was proposed twice. Simon Newcomb published a short note on it in 1881, and Frank Benford published it again, with his own collection of examples, in 1938, which is why it carries his name. Both bibliographic records are reported here, not read: Newcomb, "Note on the frequency of use of the different digits in natural numbers", American Journal of Mathematics 4, 1881; Benford, "The law of anomalous numbers", Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society 78(4), 1938, which sits behind a paywall. The formula itself, and the nine shares, are read at source in Hill's text below and computed by the engine on this page.

The figure above shows the mechanism without any of the history. Put sizes on a log ruler, where every decade has the same width. Inside a decade, the stretch from 1 to 2 takes log10(2), or 30.1 percent, of the width; the stretch from 8 to 9 takes log10(9/8), or 5.1 percent. If sizes are spread smoothly across several decades, each stretch catches trades in proportion to its width, and the first digits come out as 30.1, 17.6, 12.5, 9.7, 7.9, 6.7, 5.8, 5.1 and 4.6 percent. The law is a statement about widths on a log scale; the digits are just labels on the stretches.

The constructed tape's honest sizes are drawn to do exactly that: log-normal with a wide spread, so they run across several orders of magnitude, then rounded the way a person types a number, some to one significant digit, most to two or three. Nothing in the generator mentions Benford. Whether the tape conforms is something the page measures, not something it was told.

LiveWhy a mixture conforms and a habit does not5 min▶

Plenty of single distributions do not obey the law, which raised the obvious question of why real tables of numbers so often do. Theodore Hill answered it in 1995 ("A statistical derivation of the significant-digit law", Statistical Science 10(4); journal record reported, read at source as his 1996 preprint). His result: if distributions are picked at random and samples are taken from each, "the significant digits of the combined sample will converge to the logarithmic (Benford) distribution."

Read that as a description of a tape. Each trader is a distribution: one sizes in tenths, one in round tens, one in whatever is left in the account. A venue's tape is the combined sample, many draws from many of those distributions. Hill's theorem says that mixture lands on the law even when no individual trader's habit does. That is why a tape of many traders conforms.

It is also why a single bot does not. A wash bot is one distribution, not a mixture of many, and when it supplies a large share of the prints, the combined sample stops being a mixture of many habits and becomes mostly one habit. The round-lot bot in the lower half of the figure draws every size from seven lots, so its first digits are only ever 1, 2 or 5. A flat bot draws unrounded sizes evenly from one band of 2 to 20 units, so the digits that should be rare get as many trades as the digits that should be common. Both are a single habit; they break the law in different directions, and Part 2 shows that the direction decides how soon the test sees them.

Self-studyData that should not conform, and why it matters on a tape4 min▶

A failed first-digit test only means something if the data was expected to pass. Durtschi, Hillison and Pacini (2004, reported) list the kinds of data that should not conform: numbers that are assigned rather than chosen, such as invoice or ticket numbers; numbers confined to a bounded range; and data that does not span at least an order of magnitude. Forensic accountants learn this list before they learn the test, because running Benford on the wrong data produces confident nonsense.

Each item has a tape equivalent. A venue with a minimum and maximum order size close together publishes sizes in a bounded range. A venue that trades in fixed contract lots publishes assigned-looking sizes. And the most common trap is self-inflicted: filter a tape to trades between 10 and 20 units for some unrelated reason and almost every first digit becomes 1, honestly, by construction of the filter. Run the test on the venue's full published size column, after checking that it spans several decades, or do not run it.

Benford is one forensic test specific to a self-reported tape. General detectors for unusual values, z-scores, isolation forests and control charts, are a different family and live in learn-anomaly-detection-with-phoebe.

Part 2 · covers measuring conformity

How close is close: MAD, chi-square and a line you calibrate

13 min live

No real tape matches the law exactly; 3,000 trades are a sample, and samples wobble. The question is how far off is too far, and there are two standard ways to put a number on the distance. Harrowgate's honest Tape A gives first-digit counts of 831, 566, 366, 308, 234, 206, 192, 163 and 134, which is 27.7, 18.9, 12.2, 10.3, 7.8, 6.9, 6.4, 5.4 and 4.5 percent against the law's 30.1, 17.6, 12.5, 9.7, 7.9, 6.7, 5.8, 5.1 and 4.6.

LiveTwo measures of the same gap5 min▶

MAD is the plain one. For each digit, take observed share minus expected share, drop the sign, and average the nine. On Tape A the biggest gap is digit 1, 2.4 points under the law; digit 2 is 1.3 over; the other seven are smaller. The average is 0.0065. Because it is built from shares, MAD does not care whether the tape has 3,000 trades or 3 million; it answers "how far is the shape from the law".

Chi-square sums, over the nine digits, the squared gap between observed and expected count divided by the expected count. On Tape A it is 12.41 on 8 degrees of freedom. It answers a different question, "could a sample this size have wobbled this far by chance", and that question depends on the size: the formula is the number of trades times a weighted sum of squared share gaps, so the same shape on a tape twice as long gives twice the chi-square. On a large tape, a shape that is close enough for any practical purpose will still produce a tiny p-value. Report both, and judge with the one that answers your question.

For MAD, the reported Nigrini bands for first digits are 0.006, 0.012 and 0.015: close conformity below 0.006, acceptable to 0.012, marginal to 0.015, nonconformity above. They come from Nigrini's forensic accounting text (Wiley, 2012), reported through secondary sources whose boundary wording differs, so quote them as "the reported Nigrini bands" and never as a standard. Tape A's 0.0065 is acceptable, just over the close line.

LiveCalibrate the line on venues you trust4 min▶

Published bands were set on accounting data. A tape is not a ledger, and the honest wobble of a 3,000-trade day is a property of your data, not of anyone's textbook. So the bench measures it. Forty honest constructed venues give a mean MAD of 0.00797 with a standard deviation of 0.00125. A detector that fires at two standard deviations above the honest mean, k = 2, fires above 0.0105. The widget computes that line on load and prints it beside the reading.

Notice that the honest mean, 0.00797, already sits inside the reported "acceptable" band, not "close". A fixed threshold at the close line would complain about most honest tapes on this bench; the calibrated line does not. Session 7 measures what the calibrated line costs in false alarms on 200 fresh honest venues, which is the number that tells you what a flag is worth.

Real world

Cong, Li, Tang and Yang, "Crypto Wash Trading" (working paper read at source; published version in Management Science, reported), ran a first-significant-digit test against Benford's law as one of three tests on 29 venues' tapes from July to November 2019, beside round-size clustering and the tail of the size distribution. The authors report that their Benford-based estimates "are significantly lower" than their main ones and treat them as "somewhat lower bounds". On real tapes, as on this bench, the first-digit test is the cautious one of the three.

LiveThe naive bot is the last thing Benford sees4 min▶

Plant the round-lot washer and watch the counts in the widget: only the columns for digits 1, 2 and 5 grow. The other six are the honest tape, untouched, because every wash size is 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 or 100 units. And on Tape A, digit 1 starts 2.4 points under the law, so the first round lots added to it move that column toward the curve, not away.

The bench's detection floor, run on Tape A's partner venue in one-percent steps, shows the consequence. At 2 percent round-lot wash the roundness and clustering tests already fire (round share 32.9 percent, cluster share 14.9 percent) while the MAD reads 0.0064, silent. The MAD only crosses the 0.0105 line at 5 percent, reading 0.0107. On the same venue's ladder the MAD goes 0.0062, 0.0193, 0.0507, 0.0699 and 0.0842 at 0, 10, 30, 50 and 70 percent wash, so once the bot is large it is unmistakable. It is the order of firing that matters: of the three tests, first-digit deviation is the last to see a round-lot bot.

The flat bot moves every digit column, so its MAD climbs steadily: 0.0131 at 10 percent on the partner venue. Even so it is not the first test to catch that bot; at the default sensitivity the roundness test fires at 2 percent, clustering at 4 and the MAD only at 8 (session 7 has the floor). The first-digit test is the slowest of the three on both bots. The copy bot, which samples the honest size distribution, never moves the MAD outside the honest band at any share up to 70 percent. That is not a defect of the test; a bot that copies the mixture is a mixture, and the size column has nothing left to read. The matched buy-and-sell pairs are still on the tape, and session 7 measures exactly this limit.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

One camp uses fixed, published thresholds. Their case: a threshold everyone uses makes results comparable across teams and audits, cannot be tuned after the fact to reach a convenient answer, and carries the authority of a long forensic literature. When a regulator or an auditor asks why a tape was flagged, "it exceeded the reported Nigrini nonconformity line" is an answer anyone can check.

The other camp calibrates on its own honest population, as the bench does. Their case: the bands were set on a different kind of data, the honest spread of a tape depends on its length and its traders, and a threshold that misfires on most honest venues teaches people to ignore it. The calibration has to be done before any suspect tape is examined, on venues chosen for a stated reason, or it becomes the tuning the first camp fears.

Both camps agree that a first-digit result is a screen. Neither would publish a wash estimate from a MAD alone, and the published study above uses it as one of three tests for exactly that reason.

Part 3 · build-along

Run the first-digit test on a size column, honestly

15 min hands on

Six steps with pandas and scipy on any tape you have as a CSV with sizes stored as integer micro-units, as Harrowgate stores them since session 1. The comments quote what the constructed Tape A gives; your numbers will differ on any other tape, which is the point. Run the code in a scratch folder, never where the outputs could land in a repository.

Load the size column and check it can be tested

Integer sizes, all positive, and a spread across several decades. Data that does not span at least an order of magnitude should not conform, so if the column is that narrow, stop here: the law has nothing to say about it.

python · is this column testableimport numpy as np, pandas as pd tape = pd.read_csv("tape.csv") # columns: t, side, size_u, px sizes = tape["size_u"] # integer micro-units: 0.223 units is 223000 assert sizes.dtype.kind == "i" and (sizes > 0).all() decades = np.log10(sizes.max() / sizes.min()) print(len(sizes), "trades,", round(decades, 1), "decades") # want several, never under 1

Take the first significant digit with integer arithmetic

No floating-point log for the digit itself: a float can turn 1000 into 999.9999 and report a 9. On an integer, the first character of its decimal string is the first significant digit, exactly.

python · first digit, exactfirst = sizes.astype(str).str[0].astype(int) # 223000 -> 2, 1000000 -> 1 counts = first.value_counts().reindex(range(1, 10), fill_value=0) print(counts.tolist()) # Tape A: [831, 566, 366, 308, 234, 206, 192, 163, 134]

Shares against the law, digit by digit

The law is the only logarithm on the page. Print the gap column; it is the thing every later number summarises.

python · observed, expected, gapdigits = np.arange(1, 10) expected = np.log10(1 + 1 / digits) # 0.301, 0.176, 0.125, ... 0.046 observed = counts.to_numpy() / counts.sum() table = pd.DataFrame({"observed": observed, "benford": expected, "gap_points": (observed - expected) * 100}, index=digits) print(table.round(3)) # Tape A: digit 1 is 2.4 points under, digit 2 is 1.3 over

MAD, chi-square and the reported band, as one function

Wrap it so the calibration and the suspect tape run through exactly the same code.

python · one readingfrom scipy.stats import chisquare def benford_reading(sizes): c = sizes.astype(str).str[0].astype(int).value_counts().reindex(range(1, 10), fill_value=0) obs = c.to_numpy() / c.sum() mad = np.abs(obs - expected).mean() chi2, p = chisquare(c.to_numpy(), f_exp=expected * c.sum()) return {"counts": c.tolist(), "mad": mad, "chi2": chi2, "p": p} def reported_band(mad): # the reported Nigrini first-digit bands; wording differs by source return ("close" if mad <= 0.006 else "acceptable" if mad <= 0.012 else "marginal" if mad <= 0.015 else "nonconformity") r = benford_reading(sizes) print(round(r["mad"], 4), round(r["chi2"], 2), reported_band(r["mad"])) # Tape A: 0.0065 12.41 acceptable

Calibrate the line on tapes you trust, before you look at the suspect

Use as many honest tapes of the same length as you can justify, chosen for a reason you write down. The bench used 40. Population standard deviation, k = 2.

python · the honest linehonest = [benford_reading(pd.read_csv(f)["size_u"])["mad"] for f in honest_files] mu, sd = np.mean(honest), np.std(honest) # population sd, as the bench uses fire_above = mu + 2 * sd print(round(mu, 5), round(sd, 5), round(fire_above, 4)) # constructed bench, 40 honest venues: 0.00797 0.00125 0.0105

Now the suspect, and read the counts before the verdict

A single MAD is a summary. The counts say which digits moved, and that is what tells a round-lot bot from a flat one.

python · the suspect tapes = benford_reading(pd.read_csv("suspect.csv")["size_u"]) print(round(s["mad"], 4), "fires" if s["mad"] > fire_above else "silent", reported_band(s["mad"])) moved = np.array(s["counts"]) / sum(s["counts"]) - np.array(r["counts"]) / sum(r["counts"]) print(dict(zip(range(1, 10), (moved * 100).round(1)))) # a round-lot bot moves only 1, 2 and 5

The habit to keep. Check the column spans decades before you test it. Calibrate the line on honest tapes before you look at the suspect one. Read the nine counts, not just the MAD. And never write "conforms, therefore honest": a silent first-digit test means the sizes look like a mixture, and a round-lot bot at 2 percent and a copy bot at 70 percent both look like one.

Check yourself

Three questions

Why does a tape of many independent traders tend to conform to the first-digit law?

Hill's result does not need any single trader to follow the law; it needs many distributions, picked without bias, sampled and combined. A tape of many habits is that combination. A single bot is one distribution, which is why a large wash share breaks the mixture.

On the bench, roundness and clustering flag a round-lot washer at 2 percent of reported volume, but the MAD only crosses its line at 5 percent. Why is the first-digit test the last to fire?

Every round lot is 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 or 100 units. Six of the nine digit columns never move, and the three that do are where the law already peaks, so the shape drifts slowly. Pairs do not cancel; both legs add to the same digit. A flat bot moves every column, so its MAD climbs faster, but even there the roundness test fires first.

A venue's tape reads a MAD inside the reported acceptable band and under the calibrated line. What does that establish?

A silent first-digit test is a statement about the shape of the size column. It is not a wash estimate, it says nothing about a bot that copies honest sizes, and it does not replace the venue screening that index rulebooks apply before any weight is computed.
