Candles are a choice

Session 1 ended on a sentence: a candle is not on the tape, it is built from prints by a rule somebody chose. Tonight you choose it, on purpose, and watch what the choice does. Four fields come from four selection rules, a fifth from a sum, and all five depend on where the interval starts and ends. Build the same constructed day at one, five, fifteen and sixty minutes and the last close is 99.957 every time, while the count of up bars moves enough to flip the headline: more up bars than down at one minute, more down than up at every larger size. Somewhere in between, 169 minutes with no print at all have to be given an answer.

Part 0

Harrowgate is asked for "the 1-minute chart"

Harrowgate is a constructed data team at a fund-administration firm. Since session 1 it keeps prints at print grain, which turns out to be the easy part. On Monday a reviewer asks for "the 1-minute chart for yesterday", as if there were exactly one. To draw it, somebody has to decide where each minute starts, which clock the minute is measured on, which print is first when two share a second, and what to draw for a minute in which nothing traded. None of those decisions is on the tape. Every number below comes from the same engine as session 1, on the same constructed day of 3,000 prints, so the consequences of each decision are computed rather than asserted.

★ What you walk out with tonight. The bar rule written as five aggregations plus a count, over an interval you name. A drawing of the same fifteen minutes of prints cut three ways, so you can see a bar change colour when only the boundary moves. The constructed day at four bar sizes, computed in the page: 1,271 one-minute bars out of 1,440 possible, 169 empty, and an up-bar count that beats the down count at one minute and loses at five, fifteen and sixty. A pandas build that exposes the empty bars instead of hiding them, and a bar table that carries its own rule as columns.

LiveWhy "the 1-minute chart" is the wrong noun3 min▶

"The" implies one. There are as many 1-minute charts of a day as there are combinations of boundary, clock, tie rule and empty-bar policy, and they agree only where none of those decisions bites. On a tape with a print every few seconds they bite rarely. On a tape where 12 percent of the minutes are empty, they bite on every eighth bar.

The right noun is "a bar series built under rule R". Once the rule has a name, the reviewer's question becomes answerable, and a second reviewer who asks for five minutes gets a series that can be reconciled with the first instead of one that silently disagrees with it.

Real world

Harrowgate's first answer was a screenshot from a charting tool. The reviewer then compared it with a five-minute view of the same day and asked why one showed more up bars than down and the other the opposite. Both were correct under their own rule, and neither rule was written anywhere. Harrowgate is constructed; the conversation is common.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Bar, candle. One row summarising the prints inside one interval. A candle is the same data drawn differently.
  • OHLCV. Open, high, low, close, volume. The first four are prices picked out of the interval; the fifth is a sum of sizes.
  • Bar size. The interval length in seconds: 60, 300, 900 or 3,600 tonight.
  • Boundary. Where each interval starts. Here, aligned to midnight on the venue's clock, left-closed: a print at exactly 09:20:00 belongs to the 09:20 bar.
  • Empty bar. An interval with no print in it. Nothing was observed, so whatever the row says is a policy.
  • Carried forward. An empty bar given the previous close as its open, high, low and close, zero volume, and a flag that says so.
  • Up, down, flat bar. Close above open, below open, or equal to it. A bar with a single print is always flat.
  • Busiest bar. Tonight, the bar with the most volume. Not necessarily the one with the most prints.
Part 1 · covers building OHLCV from prints

Building a bar from prints, and where the cut falls

14 min live

A bar is six small decisions applied to a window of prints: the first one, the largest, the smallest, the last one, the sum of their sizes and how many there were. The aggregations are not controversial. The window is. Move the boundary and different prints become first and last, which changes the open and the close, which changes whether the bar is up or down, while the prints themselves have not moved at all.

LiveThe rule, field by field5 min▶

Open is the first print in the window, high the largest price, low the smallest, close the last print. Volume is the sum of the sizes, and n, the count of prints, is the column most bar tables forget and every later check needs. Open and close are order statistics, so they depend on the order of the prints; high, low, volume and n do not. That asymmetry is why the colour of a bar is the fragile part.

On the constructed tape the 09:15 five-minute bar holds 13 prints and the 09:15 fifteen-minute bar holds 37. The busiest one-minute bar by volume is 09:17, and it holds only 4 prints: busiest by volume and busiest by count are different questions, and a bar table should answer both by keeping v and n side by side.

Real world

A public market-data definition read for this session describes an open-high-low-close bar over "one unit of time, e.g., one day or one hour", and says a candlestick shows "exactly the same data" drawn differently. Volume as a fifth field, OHLCV, is a convention, reported rather than part of that definition. Neither the definition nor the convention says where the unit of time starts or what an empty one contains. That part is yours.

LiveWhere the cut falls5 min▶

Read the drawing from the top. The 37 dots are the prints between 09:15 and 09:30, placed by time and price. Cut into minutes, they make fifteen small bars, most of them flat or down. Cut into five-minute pieces, the same dots make an up bar, a down bar and an up bar. Cut once, they make a single up bar. Nothing about the trading changed between the rows. What changed is which print counts as the first and which as the last.

Three boundary decisions sit inside every bar builder. Alignment: intervals start at midnight on some clock, or at the first print, or at the moment the job started. Closure: a print at exactly 09:20:00 belongs to the bar that starts there or the one that ends there; integer division of seconds by the bar size makes it the one that starts there. Ties: two prints can share a second, and then first and last depend on the venue's sequence, not the clock. Write all three down, because a second team making a different choice will get different colours on a handful of bars and spend a week finding out why.

Self-studyA bar is a windowed aggregate4 min▶

Everything above is a tumbling window over an event stream: fixed length, no overlap, one aggregate per window. That framing is useful because it imports the right worries. A print that arrives after its window has been emitted is a late event. A window can only be closed safely once you believe nothing more is coming for it, which is a watermark decision. Re-emitting a corrected bar is an idempotency question. Those mechanics are the same for exchange prints as for clicks or sensor readings, and they are taught properly in learn-streaming-data-with-phoebe rather than here.

What is specific to a tape is the daily version of the same choice. A one-day bar needs a day boundary, and on a market that never closes the day boundary is a convention too. Leader session 1 shows four day boundaries giving opposite signs for the same day's return. The one-minute version is smaller and more frequent, and it is the one your pipeline makes thousands of times a day.

Part 2 · covers empty bars and the counts that move

Empty bars, and the count that moves with the bar

13 min live

Build the whole constructed day at four bar sizes and compare. At one minute, 1,271 of the 1,440 possible bars have at least one print and 169 have none, about 12 percent. At five minutes, fifteen minutes and an hour every bar has a print, so the empty-bar problem disappears into the larger window rather than being solved. The up count moves from 401 against 375 down at one minute, to 129 against 136 at five, 46 against 47 at fifteen and 10 against 13 at an hour. The last close is 99.957 at every size.

LiveReading the four rows5 min▶

Start on Tape A at one minute. 1,271 bars printed of 1,440 possible, 169 empty. 401 up, 375 down, 495 flat. The busiest bar by volume is 09:17 with 4 prints, the widest range is 10 ticks, and the last close is 99.957. Now switch to five minutes: 288 of 288, 129 up, 136 down, 23 flat, busiest 09:15 with 13 prints. Fifteen minutes: 96 of 96, 46 up, 47 down, 3 flat, busiest 09:15 with 37. One hour: 24 of 24, 10 up, 13 down, 1 flat, busiest 09:00 with 125 prints, and the widest range is 57 ticks, in the 04:00 bar.

Three things moved and one did not. The flat count collapses as the bar grows, because a flat bar is mostly a bar with one print, and long bars rarely have one print. The widest range grows, because a longer window has more room for the price to wander. The up-versus-down headline flips, because which print is first and last changes with the boundary. The last close stays at 99.957, because the last print of the day is the last print of the day under any rule. A dashboard that reports "share of up bars" without the bar size is reporting a fact about its configuration.

LiveAn empty minute is a decision4 min▶

The widget's bar table shows the first few slots of the day on the clock, not just the bars that printed. Where a slot is empty, it carries the previous close into open, high, low and close, writes zero volume and zero prints, and marks the row "carried". That is one honest policy. The other is to print no row at all and let the reader see the gap. The dishonest one is to carry forward silently, so 1,440 rows look like 1,440 observations.

The policy leaks into every number downstream. Carried bars are flat by construction, so on Tape A the one-minute flat count would rise by 169 if they were counted, and the up-bar share would fall without a single trade changing. Returns computed over carried closes include 169 returns of exactly zero, which pulls any volatility estimate down. The larger bars hide the problem rather than solve it: a five-minute bar that swallowed two empty minutes still has an honest open, but it no longer tells you the market was silent for part of it. Keeping n beside every bar is how that silence stays visible.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

One camp prints a row for every slot on the clock, carried forward and flagged. Their argument is alignment: every join, every cross-venue comparison and every resample assumes a regular index, and a series with holes in it breaks those quietly. The flag is the price of alignment, and it is cheap.

The other camp prints only bars that contain prints. Their argument is that a row is a claim that something was observed, and that a flag is a column most readers never filter on. On this view the gaps are information, and the job of filling them belongs to whoever needs a regular index, at the moment they need it, with their own rule.

Both camps agree on what is not acceptable: an unflagged carried bar, and a bar table that does not say which policy built it. Practitioner session 3 makes the choice harder, because four venues with four different empty minutes have to be lined up on one clock.

Part 3 · build-along

Build the bars, expose the gaps, store the rule

15 min hands on

Six steps with pandas on a constructed tape exported as a CSV with four columns: t in seconds since midnight on the venue's clock, side, size_micro as an integer number of millionths of a unit, and px_t as an integer number of ticks. The widget above prints the answers for Tape A; on any other tape, including your own, your numbers will differ, which is the point. Run the code in a scratch folder, never where the outputs could land in a repository.

Load at print grain and fix the order

Open and close depend on order, so make the order explicit. A stable sort on the clock keeps the venue's sequence for prints that share a second.

python · load and orderimport pandas as pd tape = pd.read_csv("tape.csv") # columns: t, side, size_micro, px_t tape = tape.sort_values("t", kind="stable").reset_index(drop=True) DAY = 86_400 print(len(tape), "prints")

Bucket by integer division of the clock

One line is the whole boundary rule: aligned to midnight, left-closed. A print at exactly 09:20:00 lands in the bar that starts at 09:20.

python · the boundaryBAR = 60 # seconds; try 300, 900, 3600 tape["bar"] = tape["t"] // BAR # 0 .. DAY // BAR - 1 print(tape["bar"].nunique(), "bars with at least one print")

Aggregate: first, max, min, last, sum, count

Six named aggregations. Keep n: busiest by volume and busiest by count are different bars.

python · OHLCV plus nbars = tape.groupby("bar").agg( open=("px_t", "first"), high=("px_t", "max"), low=("px_t", "min"), close=("px_t", "last"), volume_micro=("size_micro", "sum"), n=("px_t", "count")) print(bars.loc[bars["volume_micro"].idxmax()]) # the busiest bar by volume

Reindex to the full clock to expose the empty bars

groupby only returns bars that printed, so the empty ones are invisible until you ask for every slot on the clock. On Tape A at one minute this is where 169 appears.

python · every slot, flaggedbars = bars.reindex(range(DAY // BAR)) bars["observed"] = bars["n"].notna() print((~bars["observed"]).sum(), "empty bars") bars["close"] = bars["close"].ffill() # carried forward, and SAID so for col in ("open", "high", "low"): bars[col] = bars[col].fillna(bars["close"]) bars[["volume_micro", "n"]] = bars[["volume_micro", "n"]].fillna(0).astype("int64")

Count up, down and flat across bar sizes

Count only observed bars, so the policy above does not inflate the flat count. Then run it at four sizes and watch the headline.

python · the count that movesdef counts(bars): b = bars[bars["observed"]] return dict(up=int((b["close"] > b["open"]).sum()), down=int((b["close"] < b["open"]).sum()), flat=int((b["close"] == b["open"]).sum()), last_close=int(b["close"].iloc[-1])) # wrap steps 2 to 4 in build(tape, BAR), then: for size in (60, 300, 900, 3600): print(size, counts(build(tape, size)))

Store the bars with their rule as columns

The rule travels with the rows, so nobody joins a carried one-minute series to a print-only five-minute one by accident. Bars are regenerable; the prints are not, so the prints stay the source.

python · bars that say how they were builtbars["bar_sec"] = BAR bars["boundary"] = "midnight-aligned, left-closed, venue clock" bars["empty_policy"] = "carry_close_flagged" bars["bar_start_t"] = bars.index * BAR bars.to_parquet(f"bars_{BAR}s_carry.parquet") # rebuilt from prints whenever the rule changes

The habit to keep. Never say "the chart". Say "bars at 60 seconds, midnight-aligned, left-closed, carried and flagged", and keep n beside v. When two charts disagree, compare their rules before you compare their numbers.

Check yourself

Three questions

On Tape A the one-minute bars show 401 up and 375 down, and the five-minute bars show 129 up and 136 down. Why did the headline flip?

A · Five-minute bars smooth out noise, so they show the true direction of the day
B · A bar's colour compares its first and last print, and changing the boundary changes which prints are first and last; the tape did not change
C · The five-minute build dropped the 169 empty minutes, which were mostly up bars
The prints are identical in both builds and the last close is 99.957 in both. Open and close are order statistics inside a window, so moving the window moves them. Neither count is the true direction; each is a fact about a rule. The empty minutes have no prints, so they were never up or down bars in the first place.

Your one-minute job finds 169 minutes with no print. Which output is honest?

A · Interpolate a close between the neighbouring bars so the chart has no gaps
B · Either a carried row with the previous close, zero volume, zero prints and observed set to false, or no row at all, with the policy stored beside the bars
C · Carry the previous close forward so the series has 1,440 rows that all look alike
Both honest policies keep the reader able to tell an observation from a decision, and storing the policy lets the next team reproduce it. Interpolation invents prices that never printed, and a silent carry makes 169 decisions look like 169 trades.

Which statement about regenerating one object from another is right?

A · Prints can be rebuilt from one-minute bars, because the bars keep open, high, low, close and volume
B · One-minute bars can be rebuilt from five-minute bars by splitting each into five equal parts
C · Every bar size can be rebuilt from the prints; a five-minute bar can also be rebuilt from aligned one-minute bars, but never the reverse
Prints are the observation and every bar is derived from them. Larger bars can be built from smaller ones only when the boundaries nest and n and v were kept: first open, largest high, smallest low, last close, summed volume and count. A bar has already thrown away the order and the individual sizes, so nothing smaller can come back out of it.
