Measuring the detector

Three sessions built three forensic readings of a size column: the first-digit deviation, the round share and the cluster share. Session 6 planted a declared washer so there is finally something to mark them against. Tonight is the part most teams skip. Before a detector says anything about a suspect venue, it has to say three things about itself: the band it judges against, measured on honest venues only; how often that band flags an honest venue it has never seen; and the smallest washer it can actually catch. On Harrowgate's constructed bench the answers are a band from 40 honest venues, 3 of 200 fresh honest venues flagged at the default sensitivity, a floor of 2 percent of reported volume for one bot and 4 percent for another, and one careful bot that no size test will ever see.

Part 0

Harrowgate has three detectors and no idea how often they cry wolf

Harrowgate is a constructed data team at a fund-administration firm, and it now owns three forensic detectors and a declared wash trader to test them on. The temptation is to point the detectors at the venue table and start flagging. The trouble is that nobody can yet answer the first question a reviewer will ask about any flag: how often does this thing fire on a venue that is doing nothing wrong? A detector that cannot answer that is not a detector, it is an opinion with a threshold. So tonight the detectors are measured before they are used, on the same constructed engine as every other bench, with every number computed in the page.

The false-positive table at four sensitivities, printed live: 1.5 percent at k=2, and the honest reading of "0 of 200" above it. The detection floor for two bots, and the surprise that the first-digit test is the last of the three to fire on both. And the copy bot, which no size test catches at any share up to 70 percent, stated as the limit it is.

LiveA detector is three numbers, not one3 min▶

The reading is what the detector computes on a venue: a first-digit deviation of 0.0193, a round share of 44.9 percent. On its own it means nothing, because nobody knows what an honest venue reads.

The false-positive rate is how often the detector's rule flags a venue that is honest. It has to be measured on honest venues the band was not fitted on, and it has to be printed beside every flag, because a reviewer weighing a flag needs to know how cheap a flag is.

The floor is the smallest planted washer the rule catches. Below it the detector is silent, and silence below the floor is not evidence of honesty. A detector without a stated floor invites the reading "we checked and it was clean" when the truth is "we checked with something that cannot see that small".

Real world

Cong, Li, Tang and Yang, "Crypto Wash Trading" (NBER working paper w30783, read at source), do the same thing at research scale: they estimate the benchmark relation between round and unrounded volume on the regulated venues in their 2019 sample, and only then measure the unregulated venues against it. They also state their bounds out loud: their Benford-based estimates are significantly lower and are offered as somewhat lower bounds, and if genuine algorithmic trading inflates unrounded volume, their roundness estimates should be read more as upper bounds. That is a published estimate carrying its own floor and ceiling, which is the standard tonight sets for a detector.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Calibration set. The honest venues the band is fitted on: 40 constructed tapes, seeds 1001 to 1040. Nothing washed ever enters it.
  • k. The sensitivity, in honest standard deviations. A reading fires when it sits more than k spreads from the honest mean.
  • Band. The range a reading may take without firing. One-sided for the first-digit deviation, two-sided for roundness and clustering.
  • Fires. One detector's reading left its band.
  • Flagged. At least two of the three detectors fired on the same venue. The majority rule is the verdict.
  • False-positive rate. The share of fresh honest venues flagged: 200 constructed tapes, seeds 2001 to 2200, never used for calibration.
  • Floor. The smallest declared wash share, in one-percent steps, at which the rule flags the planted venue.
  • Ceiling statement. What "0 of 200" really says: none were seen in 200 trials, so the rate is probably small, and 200 trials cannot say how small.
Part 1 · covers calibration and the band

Calibrate on honest venues, never on the target

14 min live

A band is a description of honest. It is built by generating 40 honest constructed tapes, taking the three readings on each, and recording the mean and the spread of each reading across the 40. The band at sensitivity k is the mean plus or minus k spreads. Then, and only then, a second pile of 200 fresh honest tapes is judged against the finished band to count false positives. The washed tape sits on the far side of a wall: it is judged by the band and never helps make it.

LiveWhy the target never enters the calibration5 min▶

Suppose Harrowgate calibrated on its whole venue table, suspects included. A venue with a round-lot washer at 30 percent reads a round share of 67.0 percent on the constructed bench, against an honest mean of 29.96. Put that venue in the calibration set and two things happen at once: the mean moves towards it, and the spread widens to cover it. The band now describes "venues like the ones we have", washers included, and the detector has been taught that the thing it hunts is normal. The more washers in the table, the more completely they hide.

The same mistake has a quieter form: calibrating on the full table and then dropping "the obvious outliers" before computing the band. That is calibrating on the target with an extra step, because the definition of "obvious" was the detector's own judgement. The only clean calibration set is one chosen for a reason that has nothing to do with the readings: on the bench, tapes generated honest by declaration; in practice, a reference set picked on criteria written down before any reading was taken.

LiveOne side for first digits, two sides for sizes5 min▶

The three bands are not built the same way. The first-digit deviation is one-sided: a tape cannot be "too Benford", and more deviation is the only direction that means anything, so the MAD fires above the mean plus k spreads. Roundness and clustering are two-sided, and this is the design choice that matters most. A naive bot types round lots and pushes both readings up. But the published finding on unregulated venues was the other direction: Cong, Li, Tang and Yang report that regulated venues show an evident clustering effect at round sizes while unregulated venues show little, so in their sample the anomaly is too little roundness. A one-sided round test would have missed exactly what they found.

At k=2 on the constructed bench the bands are:

  • First-digit MAD fires above 0.0105.
  • Round share fires outside 28.3 to 31.6 percent.
  • Cluster share fires outside 10.8 to 13.5 percent.
  • Flagged when at least two of the three fire.

Raise k and every band widens: at k=2.5 the edges are 0.0111, 27.9 to 32.0 and 10.5 to 13.8; at k=3 they are 0.0117, 27.5 to 32.5 and 10.2 to 14.2; at k=4, 0.0130, 26.6 to 33.3 and 9.5 to 14.8. A wider band fires less on honest venues and sees less of the washer. Part 2 prints both sides of that trade.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree: whose honest?4 min▶

On the bench, "honest" is declared: the generator made those 40 tapes without a washer, so they are honest by construction. Outside the bench nobody hands you that set, and the choice of reference is where the argument lives.

One camp uses the venues with the most outside scrutiny as the reference, the way the published study used its three regulated venues to estimate the benchmark round-to-unrounded relation. The case for it: those venues carry obligations and supervision the others do not, so they are the best available picture of what human order flow looks like.

The other camp points out that a reference set is honest only on the readings you happen to test, that heavily supervised venues may attract a different mix of traders altogether, and that the same study warns its roundness estimates become upper bounds if genuine algorithmic trading produces unrounded volume. On this view the reference is a modelling choice to be stress-tested, not a ground truth. Both camps agree on the part that matters tonight: the reference is chosen before the readings, on stated criteria, and it never contains the venue being judged.

Part 2 · covers the false-positive rate and the floor

The false-positive rate, printed

13 min live

The band is finished. Now 200 fresh honest constructed tapes, seeds 2001 to 2200, each one never seen by the calibration, are read and judged at four sensitivities. The count of honest venues flagged is the false-positive rate, and it is a measurement, not a promise. Below it, the other half of the trade: the smallest planted washer the same rule can catch.

LiveRead the count, then read the ceiling5 min▶

At the default k=2 the majority rule flags 3 of the 200 fresh honest venues: 1.5 percent. That is the number to print beside every flag Harrowgate raises. If the venue table has 40 honest venues in it, a 1.5 percent rate says to expect a flag on an innocent venue now and then, and a reviewer should treat a single flag as a reason to look, not a finding.

Move k to 2.5 and the count drops to 0 of 200. The tempting sentence is "no false positives at k=2.5". It is wrong. "0 of 200" is a ceiling statement about 200 trials: none were seen, so the true rate is probably small, and 200 honest venues are not enough to say how small. 3 of 200 has the same problem in the other direction; 1.5 percent is the point estimate, and with 200 trials the uncertainty around it is wide. The build-along computes that interval on your own run, so the number is yours rather than quoted.

LiveThe majority rule is quieter than any single test4 min▶

Look at the per-detector rows at k=2. The first-digit test alone fires on 13 honest venues, 6.5 percent. Roundness alone fires on 7, 3.5 percent; clustering on 5, 2.5 percent. Yet the rule that needs two of the three flags only 3. Honest venues that wander out of one band mostly stay inside the other two, so asking for agreement buys a quieter verdict without moving any band.

The effect holds as k rises: at k=2.5 the MAD still fires on 8 honest venues, 4.0 percent, and roundness on 3, while the majority rule flags none. At k=3 only the MAD fires, on 3 venues, 1.5 percent. At k=4 nothing fires. The single noisiest test is the first-digit one, which is worth remembering when someone proposes running it alone because it is the famous one.

LiveThe floor: the smallest washer the rule can see4 min▶

The floor is found the dull way, which is the right way: plant the bot into venue B (seed 12) at 1 percent of reported volume, then 2, then 3, and judge each tape against the same band. At 1 percent nothing fires for either bot. The round-lot bot is flagged from 2 percent: roundness reads 32.9 and clustering 14.9, both above their bands, while the first-digit deviation sits at 0.0064, comfortably honest. The MAD does not join until 5 percent, where 0.0107 crosses the 0.0105 line.

The flat bot is slower to catch. At 2 percent roundness fires alone, at 27.6, below the band, because the bot's unrounded four-digit sizes dilute the round share. Clustering joins at 4 percent, at 10.5, and that second detector is what flags the venue. The MAD only fires at 8 percent, at 0.0111. Raise k and the flat floor climbs, to 5 percent at k=2.5 and 6 percent at k=3, while the round floor stays at 2 percent at every k up to 3. That is the price of the quieter false-positive count, printed rather than assumed.

So Harrowgate's honest statement at k=2 is: "flagged venues are flagged by a rule that fires on 1.5 percent of honest venues, and venues with less than about 2 percent round-lot wash, or less than about 4 percent flat-size wash, would not be flagged at all."

Self-studyWhy the first-digit test fires last4 min▶

The first-digit law is the famous test, and on this bench it is the least sensitive of the three on both bots. The round-lot bot cycles 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100 units, so its first digits are 1, 2 and 5, and Benford's law already expects a first digit of 1 about 30.1 percent of the time and 2 about 17.6 percent. The bot's digits land where honest digits are already plentiful, and it takes a large dose before the mix bends far enough to notice.

The flat bot draws from 2 to 20 units, so its first digits run across the whole range and partly blend in, while its sizes carry four significant digits that almost nobody types. The habit that gives it away is about how many digits, not which first digit, and that is what roundness and clustering read. The lesson generalises: a detector is sensitive to the property it measures and nothing else, and the famous one is not automatically the useful one. Hill's result explains why an honest tape of many traders conforms; it says nothing about how fast a small bot breaks the conformity.

Part 3 · build-along

Calibrate, count the false alarms, find the floor

15 min hands on

Six steps with numpy, pandas and scipy. You need two folders of honest tapes as CSVs with the columns t, side, size_u and px (sizes as integer micro-units), 40 for calibration and 200 more for the count, and the plant() function from session 6. Your random draws will differ from the engine's, so your numbers will differ; the comments quote only the constructed bench's figures for comparison. Run it in a scratch folder, never where the outputs could land in a repository.

One function for all three readings

The same function for the calibration set, the fresh set and the suspect. Integer sizes, so the round flag and first digit are exact.

python · three readingsimport numpy as np, pandas as pd from pathlib import Path BENFORD = np.log10(1 + 1 / np.arange(1, 10)) # 30.1, 17.6, 12.5 ... 4.6 percent def is_round(m: int) -> bool: while m > 0 and m % 10 == 0: m //= 10 return m < 10 # one significant digit left def readings(sizes: pd.Series) -> dict: first = sizes.astype(str).str[0].astype(int) obs = first.value_counts(normalize=True).reindex(range(1, 10), fill_value=0).to_numpy() vc = sizes.value_counts().sort_index().sort_values(ascending=False, kind="stable") return {"mad": float(np.abs(obs - BENFORD).mean()), "round": float(sizes.map(is_round).mean()), "cluster": float(vc.head(5).sum() / len(sizes))}

Calibrate on the honest folder, and nothing else

Assert the folder holds what you think it holds. Population standard deviation, as the bench uses.

python · the bandcalib_files = sorted(Path("honest_calibration").glob("*.csv")) assert len(calib_files) == 40 # honest by construction; no suspect in here cal = pd.DataFrame([readings(pd.read_csv(f)["size_u"]) for f in calib_files]) mu, sd = cal.mean(), cal.std(ddof=0) print(mu.round(5).to_dict(), sd.round(5).to_dict()) # constructed bench: MAD 0.00797 sd 0.00125, round 0.2996 sd 0.0084, cluster 0.1217 sd 0.0067

z-scores, one side for MAD, two sides for sizes, two of three to flag

Print the band edges at each k so the rule is written down where a reviewer can read it.

python · the verdictdef verdict(r: dict, k: float) -> dict: z = (pd.Series(r) - mu) / sd fired = {"mad": bool(z["mad"] > k), # one-sided: more deviation is the anomaly "round": bool(abs(z["round"]) > k), # two-sided: too little roundness counts too "cluster": bool(abs(z["cluster"]) > k)} return {"z": z.round(2).to_dict(), "fired": fired, "flagged": sum(fired.values()) >= 2} for k in (2, 2.5, 3, 4): lo, hi = mu - k * sd, mu + k * sd print(k, "MAD above", round(hi["mad"], 4), "round", round(lo["round"] * 100, 1), "to", round(hi["round"] * 100, 1), "cluster", round(lo["cluster"] * 100, 1), "to", round(hi["cluster"] * 100, 1)) # constructed bench, k=2: MAD above 0.0105, round 28.3 to 31.6, cluster 10.8 to 13.5

Count false positives on fresh honest venues, and say how uncertain the count is

Read the fresh folder once, judge it at every k. Then compute an exact binomial interval on your own counts. The interval is your output, not a figure from this page; read the upper end of the zero row and notice it is not zero.

python · the false-positive tablefrom scipy.stats import binomtest fresh_files = sorted(Path("honest_fresh").glob("*.csv")) assert not set(fresh_files) & set(calib_files) # never a calibration venue fresh = [readings(pd.read_csv(f)["size_u"]) for f in fresh_files] # read once n = len(fresh) for k in (2, 2.5, 3, 4): v = [verdict(r, k) for r in fresh] per = {d: sum(x["fired"][d] for x in v) for d in ("mad", "round", "cluster")} hits = sum(x["flagged"] for x in v) ci = binomtest(hits, n).proportion_ci(confidence_level=0.95, method="exact") print(k, per, hits, "of", n, "| 95 percent interval", round(ci.low * 100, 2), "to", round(ci.high * 100, 2), "percent") # constructed bench, k=2: mad 13, round 7, cluster 5, flagged 3 of 200 # constructed bench, k=2.5 and above: flagged 0 of 200, a ceiling statement, never "zero"

Find the floor in one-percent steps

Plant each bot with session 6's plant() at 1, 2, 3 ... 8 percent, judge each tape against the same band, and stop at the first flag. Print which detectors fired at each step, because the order they join in is half the finding.

python · the floor searchdef floor(style: str, k: float, steps=range(1, 9)): for pct in steps: tape = plant(pct / 100, style) # session 6: declared share of reported volume v = verdict(readings(tape["size_u"]), k) print(style, k, pct, [d for d, f in v["fired"].items() if f]) if v["flagged"]: return pct return None # not seen within the steps tried: say so for k in (2, 2.5, 3): print("k", k, "round floor", floor("round", k), "flat floor", floor("flat", k)) # constructed venue B (seed 12): round 2 percent at every k up to 3; flat 4, 5 and 6 percent

Meet the honest limit: the copy bot

Plant the copy bot, which samples the honest size distribution, at up to 70 percent. Every size reading stays inside the band. The matched pairs are still on the tape; nothing in readings() looks at time or side.

python · the limit, statedfor w in (0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7): tape = plant(w, "copy") r = readings(tape["size_u"]) v = verdict(r, 2) print(w, round(r["round"] * 100, 1), round(r["cluster"] * 100, 1), round(r["mad"], 4), sum(v["fired"].values()), "fired") # constructed venue B at 70 percent: round 30.7, cluster 12.6, MAD 0.0074, 0 fired
Self-studyThe copy bot is the limit, not a failure4 min▶

On the constructed bench the copy bot is never caught at any share up to 70 percent at k=2. At 70 percent its venue reads a round share of 30.7, a cluster share of 12.6 and a MAD of 0.0074, every one inside the honest band, and 0 detectors fire. That is not a bug in the detectors. A washer that draws its sizes from the honest size distribution produces an honest size distribution, by construction, and every test that reads only sizes will call it honest. Writing that down is part of stating the detector.

What the copy bot cannot hide is its structure: a buy and a sell of the same size, one to four seconds apart, over and over. Session 6 planted exactly that and asserted it on every tape. None of the three detectors reads timestamps or sides, so none of them can see it. A detector that does would need its own calibration on honest venues, because honest traders also reverse positions quickly, and its own printed false-positive rate. The general machinery for building and validating detectors of that kind, z-scores, isolation forests and control charts, lives in learn-anomaly-detection-with-phoebe; tonight's homework sketches the first one on this bench.

The habit to keep. State every detector as three numbers: the band, the false-positive rate with the number of honest trials behind it, and the floor with the bot it was measured on. Calibrate on a reference chosen before any reading was taken, and never on the venue being judged. Say "0 of 200", never "no false positives". And write down what the detector cannot read, because the washer that knows will live exactly there.

Check yourself

Three questions

Why is the band calibrated on 40 honest venues only, instead of on every venue in Harrowgate's table?

The band is a description of honest. Fit it on the target and the target shapes its own judge: a venue reading 67.0 percent round pulls the mean up and the spread out, and the more washers in the table, the more completely they hide. The honest venues are chosen by declaration, before any reading, and the washed tape is judged by a band it never touched.

At k=2.5 the majority rule flags 0 of 200 fresh honest venues. What does that tell you?

"0 of 200" is a ceiling statement about 200 trials, never "zero false positives". An exact binomial interval on 0 of 200 has an upper end above zero, which the build-along computes on your own run. And the detector still catches washers at k=2.5: the round floor stays at 2 percent and the flat floor moves to 5.

The copy bot is never flagged at any share up to 70 percent. Why?

At 70 percent the copy bot's venue reads 30.7 / 12.6 / 0.0074, inside every band, so 0 detectors fire. The share is enormous and the calibration is fine; the detectors read sizes and nothing else. This is the honest limit of size-based detection, stated as part of the detector, not a failure of it.

Before session 8

Homework

About forty minutes, split between the bench and your own work.

Sketch a pair reader and measure it like the others

Count, per venue, the trades that have an opposite-side trade of the same size within 4 seconds, as a share of all trades. Calibrate that share on the 40 honest tapes, count how often it fires on the 200 fresh ones, then run it on the copy bot. Write the three numbers down. Expect honest venues to produce some pairs by chance; that is exactly why the calibration comes first.

State one detector your team already runs as three numbers

Any alert, any threshold, any rule. What reference was its threshold set on, and did that reference contain the things it hunts? How often does it fire on cases known to be clean, and out of how many? What is the smallest problem it would catch? If any of the three is unknown, that is the finding; bring it.

Three numbers. A detector is its reading, its false-positive rate on honest venues it never saw, and its floor. One number is an opinion.

The verdict. Flagged when at least two of three fire. Quieter than any single test: 1.5 percent against 6.5 for the MAD alone at k=2.
The count. 3 of 200 honest venues at k=2. Print it beside every flag, with the number of trials.
The ceiling. "0 of 200" means none seen in 200, never "zero false positives". Compute the interval; its upper end is not zero.
The floor. Round-lot bot from 2 percent, flat bot from 4 at k=2. Raise k and the flat floor climbs to 5, then 6.
The limit. A copy bot reads honest on every size test at up to 70 percent. Say so; the pairs need a different reader.
