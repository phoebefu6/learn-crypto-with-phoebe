From warehouse to crypto desk: the transfer map

Five sessions took the conventional market-data feed apart: no close, volume that reports itself, many prices at once, a unit that moves, and no correction ever arriving. Tonight turns that into something a person can act on in the morning. A data analyst, engineer or scientist from a warehouse team is not starting from zero on a crypto desk. Some of what they already know is worth more here than it was before, some of it is new, and a handful of habits are actively dangerous. Three columns, each skill drawn landing on the layer of the crypto data stack where it does its work, four habits run live on constructed tapes, and a personal map you fill in the room with a 90-day reading ladder behind it. Nothing here is investment advice, and nothing here is a real venue, asset or price.

Part 0

Harrowgate, the month three warehouse people joined the desk

Harrowgate is a constructed data team at a fund-administration firm, the one that publishes a daily reference level and a venue volume table for an abstract asset. It has just hired three people from a conventional warehouse team: an analyst who owned the daily reports, an engineer who owned the loads and the checks, and a scientist who owned the models and the alerts. All three are good. In their first week each one does something that would have been exactly right on their old feed and is quietly wrong here. Tonight is the map that would have told them which of their instincts to keep, which to add, and which to stop trusting.

LiveWhy a map and not a reading list3 min▶

People who move from a warehouse team to market data usually get a reading list. It is the wrong shape. A list tells you what to add and says nothing about what to keep or what to drop, and the dropping is where the damage happens: the habits that hurt here are the ones that felt like competence on the old feed, so nobody thinks to question them.

A transfer map has three columns because a career move has three kinds of content. What carries over is the part you should be confident about, and it is larger than most people expect. What must be learned is new vocabulary and new tests. What must be unlearned is a set of sentences that were true on the old feed, "there is a close", "volume is a fact", "one asset, one price", "rank by size", "the source is audited", and are false here in ways sessions 1 to 5 measured.

Real world

One public index rulebook, read at source, shows what a mature practitioner keeps and what they add. It keeps the conventional machinery: a fixed calculation window, partitions, medians and time weights. It adds a step no warehouse pipeline has: venues are screened quarterly on criteria such as "Regulated by an independent government body" and "KYC/AML controls", plus a liquidity floor, before any of their trades count. The arithmetic carried over; the trust in the source did not.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Transfer map. Three columns, carries over, must learn, must unlearn, with each item placed on the layer of the data stack where it does its work. Tonight.
  • The crypto data stack. Five layers a reported number passes through: prints and time, reported volume, venues to a level, weights and ranks, units and labels.
  • Forensic statistics. Tests that read the shape of numbers somebody reported about themselves: the first-digit law, roundness, clustering. Practitioner sessions 4 and 5.
  • Calibration. Measuring how often a test fires on honest data before trusting it on anything else. Practitioner session 7.
  • Venue reconciliation. Turning many venues' prints into one level, with stale and outlier venues excluded by a stated rule. Session 3 and practitioner session 3.
  • Restatement. Dividing a quoted price by the quoting unit's own level so a move in the ruler is not read as a move in the asset. Session 4.
  • Reported label. An attribute somebody else asserted, such as "this address belongs to that venue", carried into a join without being verified. Practitioner session 9.
  • Official source. A document published by the body that sets or owns the rule it describes. On the ladder below these are marked; everything else is free but not official.
Part 1 · covers what carries over

The map, and the four skills that are worth more here

12 min live

Start with the good news, because it is large. The arithmetic of market data does not change on a crypto desk: bars are bars, medians are medians, a reconciliation is a reconciliation. What changes is that nobody else guarantees the inputs any more, and a skill that used to be a nice habit becomes the only guarantee the team has. The map below puts every item from tonight on one page. Read the left column first.

LiveFour skills, and the layer each one lands on5 min▶

OHLCV and time-series modelling lands on layer 1, prints and time. Anyone who has built bars from events already has grain discipline: one row is one print, a candle is an aggregate, and you know which you are looking at. On a crypto tape that skill is used every hour. Practitioner session 2 rebuilds candles from Tape A and finds 1,271 one-minute bars printed out of 1,440 possible, 169 minutes with no trade at all, so "the 1-minute close" at those minutes is a convention you choose, carry forward or print nothing. A warehouse person who already asks "what does an empty bucket mean" is ahead of most.

Reconciliation and data-quality tests land on layers 2 and 5. The habit that says two sources disagreeing is a finding, not a bug to suppress, is the single most valuable instinct on a feed where every venue reports itself. The engineer's reflex to put a check on every load becomes the place a forensic screen lives. Practitioner session 9 is where the reconciliation instinct meets a join whose key is a reported label.

Index construction lands on layer 3. Weighted averages, medians, partitions and exclusion rules are exactly the machinery of a reference level. Session 3 ran four defensible methods on the same four constructed venues at 13:00 and got four levels 75.5 ticks apart before any exclusion; a person who has built a composite index before knows to write the method beside the number.

Anomaly detection lands on layer 4. Calibration before judgement, the idea that a threshold means nothing until you know how often it fires on clean data, carries over whole. The general detectors live in learn-anomaly-detection-with-phoebe; the forensic ones are new and sit in the next column.

Real world

The public index rulebook read for these sessions is index construction a warehouse person would recognise on sight: a fixed 3,600-second calculation window split into 10 partitions of 6 minutes, a volume-median trade per partition described as "outlier resistant by nature", and time weights from 1/10 to 10/10 favouring the recent partitions. Nothing in that list is crypto-specific. It is the skill, carried over, applied to an input nobody guarantees.

LiveWhy they are worth more here, not less4 min▶

On a conventional feed a lot of the team's discipline was insurance against something that rarely happened. The operator counted the volume, the market closed, the print got corrected when it was wrong. A sloppy team could get away with a lot, because the arrangement caught its mistakes.

On a crypto desk there is no arrangement to catch them. Session 5 drew the loop that never closes: no authority ever publishes a venue's true wash share, so a detector's accuracy can only be measured on a bench where the truth was planted. On the constructed bench, 3 of 200 fresh honest venues are flagged at k=2, 1.5 percent, after a band set on 40 other honest venues. That number exists only because somebody on the team insisted on calibration before judgement. The warehouse habit became the only guarantee.

The fourth carried skill is stream thinking: late prints, watermarks, idempotent loads. A venue tape is an event stream like any other, and the mechanics are the same ones learn-streaming-data-with-phoebe teaches. Nothing about them needs relearning; they just never get a day off, because the stream never stops.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

One camp holds that the transfer is mostly method. A good analyst, engineer or scientist brings grain discipline, reconciliation and calibration, and the market-specific vocabulary takes a few weeks. On this view a desk should hire for method and teach the domain, and the map's left column is the one that matters.

The other camp holds that the domain is where the risk lives. Someone who does not know that venues report their own volume, or that the quoting unit can move, will apply excellent method to the wrong input and produce a confident wrong number. On this view the right-hand column, the habits to unlearn, is the expensive one, and method without it is a liability.

Both camps agree on the map's shape: the left column is real and larger than newcomers expect, and the right-hand column cannot be skipped. Where they differ is which column they would test first in an interview.

LiveForensic statistics: reading a number its author wrote5 min▶

The biggest new item is a family of tests a warehouse team rarely needs, because its numbers were counted by someone with no reason to lie. Here each venue counts itself. Forensic statistics read the shape of the reported numbers rather than their size. The first-digit law says that in data mixing many sources across several orders of magnitude, the leading digit is 1 about 30.1 percent of the time, 2 about 17.6 percent, falling to 4.6 percent for 9. Hill's 1995 result, read at source in his 1996 preprint, is why: samples drawn from many different distributions converge on that pattern when combined. A tape of many traders with many habits conforms. One bot with one habit does not.

On constructed Tape A the honest venue's mean absolute deviation from the law is 0.0065, inside the "acceptable" band of the reported Nigrini thresholds and close to the 0.006 "close conformity" line. Roundness and clustering join it: the share of one-significant-digit sizes and the share of trades at the five most common sizes. A venue is flagged when two of the three fire. Practitioner sessions 4 and 5 build all three.

The detail newcomers miss is the direction. Cong, Li, Tang and Yang (read at source) found that on their 2019 sample the regulated venues showed clear clustering at round sizes and the unregulated ones showed little: the anomaly was too little roundness, not too much. So the bands are two-sided. The widget above shows the cost of trusting a screen without its limit: a round-lot washer fires 3 of 3, and a washer that copies honest sizes fires 0 of 3 at the same weight.

LiveVenues, time and the unit: three new reflexes4 min▶

Venue reconciliation. There is no consolidated tape, so a level is something the team constructs. On session 3's constructed market at 13:00, venue D's last print is 392 seconds old and 0.28 percent from the median, outside a 0.2 percent tolerance. Leave it in and the simple mean is 99.7455; take the median with the rules on and the level is 99.670. The new reflex is not the median, which a warehouse person already knows. It is asking which venues are allowed in and writing the rule beside the number.

24/7 time handling. No bell, so every daily number carries a boundary somebody chose. Session 1's five-day tape gives a first full-day return of +0.052 percent at midnight, -0.020 at 08:00, -0.061 at 16:00 and +0.054 at 21:00. The skill is small and permanent: the boundary goes in the same cell as the number.

Unit-of-account risk. Prices are quoted in a unit that is often itself a traded asset, the thing the market calls a stablecoin, and it can slip. On session 4's constructed 6 percent depeg the quoted tape's worst hour is -0.046 percent and the restated one is -3.96 percent. The prints did not change; the ruler did. The new habit is storing the unit's own level at the grain of the prints, so restatement is a join rather than a project.

LiveChain-to-exchange joins, where the key is somebody's claim3 min▶

Sooner or later someone asks to join a venue's flows to chain data. The engineer reaches for a join key, finds a label that says "this address belongs to that venue", and joins on it. The new knowledge is that the label is a reported attribute, asserted by whoever published it, not a resolved identity. The join is fine; reporting its output as fact is not. Practitioner session 9 builds the join and measures its coverage.

The chain side belongs to sibling sessions and is not re-taught here. What addresses can and cannot say about identity is in learn-web3-with-phoebe; how a chain works is in learn-blockchain-with-phoebe; and deciding whether two records are the same party is learn-entity-resolution-with-phoebe.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree: 77.5 against 954 min▶

Anyone learning forensic statistics will meet two numbers and be tempted to pick one. Cong, Li, Tang and Yang (read at source) estimate wash trading at 77.5 percent on average of the reported volume on unregulated venues, median 79.1, across 26 unregulated venues over about four months of 2019, using a benchmark of round-versus-unrounded volume set on 3 regulated venues. They bound it both ways: Benford-based estimates come out lower, and if genuine algorithmic trading inflates unrounded volume their figures are closer to upper bounds.

An industry presentation by Fusaro and Hougan in March 2019, reported rather than read, put about 95 percent of one widely used aggregator's reported volume for one asset as fake or non-economic, across 81 venues over four days, using trade-size histograms, volume patterns and spread behaviour, and found 10 venues with real volume. Different denominators, samples, windows and methods. Cong and co-authors say their estimates are in the same order of magnitude as the industry reports.

Neither number is "the" wash rate, and no authority will ever publish the true one. The skill to learn is not which to believe. It is carrying both with their denominators, and saying that the venues may have adapted since 2019.

Part 3 · covers what must be unlearned, then the build-along

Five sentences that were true on the old feed, and your own map

18 min live and hands on

The right-hand column is the expensive one, because every habit in it feels like competence. Each is a sentence a warehouse team never had to say out loud, because the arrangement made it true. Here each one prints a number that looks finished and is not. Six minutes on the five, then you fill your own map.

LiveWhy these five, and why they are hard to drop4 min▶

Every one of the five was correct on the old feed. "There is a close" was true because the market stopped. "Volume is a fact" was true because an operator with an auditor counted it. "One asset, one price" was true because there was a consolidated tape. "Rank by size" was reasonable because size was measured, not claimed. "The source is audited" was true, and it quietly underwrote the other four. That is what makes them dangerous: they are not mistakes a newcomer makes, they are the professional judgement of a good warehouse team applied to a feed that no longer supports it.

Run the widget in Part 2 on Tape B with "volume is a fact" and the washed venue sits at rank 1 by reported volume; session 2 showed it moving from rank 2 to rank 1 at only 10 percent wash. A league table moves before a chart looks strange. The replacement is not to stop weighting by volume. Volume weighting is standard in every index rulebook read for these sessions, and correct when the volume is real; the rulebooks screen which venues may contribute before any weight is computed.

One honest limit belongs on the unlearn list too: "the screen caught it, so the venue is clean". A washer that copies the honest size distribution passes all three tests at 70 percent wash. What still gives it away is the matched-pair structure, same size, opposite side, seconds apart, and these three tests do not read it.

Self-studyHow to tell a habit from a skill3 min▶

A useful test for anything on your own map: does it depend on someone else guaranteeing the input? Rebuilding candles from prints does not, so it is a skill and carries over. Taking the close from the feed does, so it is a habit, and it goes in the right-hand column. Reconciling two sources does not. Trusting one source because it is the official one does.

The test sorts most cases in a second. The ones it leaves ambiguous, such as "use a median", are skills whose inputs changed: the median carries over, the decision about which venues are allowed into it is new. Those go in the left column with a note pointing at the middle one.

The build-along. No code. Draw three columns and five rows on paper, one row per layer of the stack, and fill it from the job you do today. The role steps below are prompts, not answers; your map will not match anyone else's, and should not.

Draw the grid and name the layers

Columns: carries over, must learn, must unlearn. Rows: prints and time, reported volume, venues to a level, weights and ranks, units and labels. Leave every cell empty for now. The empty grid is the point: it shows you which layers your current job never touched.

If you are an analyst today

Carries over: building daily and period tables, explaining a change, reconciling two reports. Must learn first: 24/7 time handling and unit restatement, because both change the numbers you publish without changing the prints. Must unlearn first: "there is a close" and "volume is a fact". Sessions 1, 2 and 4, then practitioner session 2.

If you are an engineer today

Carries over: loads, data-quality checks, idempotency, stream thinking. Must learn first: venue reconciliation and chain-to-exchange joins whose key is a reported label. Must unlearn first: "the source is audited" and "one asset, one price". Session 3, then practitioner sessions 1, 3 and 9.

If you are a scientist today

Carries over: time-series modelling, anomaly detection, calibration before judgement. Must learn first: forensic statistics, calibrated on honest venues, with the false-positive rate and the case the method cannot catch stated together. Must unlearn first: "rank by size", and trusting any detector that has no bench. Practitioner sessions 4 to 7, then the tape bench in practitioner session 10.

Put one session in every filled cell

Each cell you filled gets a pointer: the session that measured it, or a sibling course if it is a seam. A cell with no pointer is a belief, not a plan. Circle the two right-hand cells you are most likely to fall back on under pressure; those are your first month.

Days 1 to 30 · prints, bars and time

Read the pandas user guide sections on time-series resampling and on groupby (official, the library's own documentation). Work practitioner sessions 1 and 2 and leader session 1. Revisit late data and watermarks in learn-streaming-data-with-phoebe (free, by Phoebe Fu, not official). Exit test: rebuild a candle table and state its boundary and its empty-bar rule in one sentence.

Days 31 to 60 · reported volume and reconciliation

Read Hill's 1996 preprint of "A statistical derivation of the significant-digit law" (free, read at source for these sessions) and Cong, Li, Tang and Yang, "Crypto Wash Trading", NBER working paper w30783 (free; read sections 3 and 4 and the authors' own caveats). Pick any public index provider's methodology rulebook and read its partition, weighting and venue-screening sections (official for that index). Work practitioner sessions 3 to 7, with learn-anomaly-detection-with-phoebe for the general detectors. Exit test: say the false-positive rate of your screen and the case it cannot catch.

Days 61 to 90 · units, joins and the rulebook above the desk

Read IOSCO's "Policy Recommendations for Crypto and Digital Asset Markets", Final Report FR11/2023, 16 November 2023 (official, from the international securities standard setter; non-binding), for its treatment of market manipulation, conflicts at vertically integrated venues, custody and surveillance; IOSCO reported a thematic review of implementation in October 2025. Work practitioner sessions 8, 9 and 10, and hand the chain side to learn-web3-with-phoebe, learn-blockchain-with-phoebe and learn-entity-resolution-with-phoebe. Exit test: redraw your map from memory and move at least one cell.

The habit to keep. Before you trust an instinct on a crypto desk, ask whether it depends on someone else guaranteeing the input. If it does, it belongs in the right-hand column until you have replaced the guarantee yourself: a stated boundary, a calibrated screen, a reconciliation rule, a restated unit, a label marked as reported.

Check yourself

Three questions

A new Harrowgate engineer asks which of their warehouse habits is worth more on the crypto desk than it was before. Which one?

Reconciliation carries over and becomes the team's own guarantee, because no operator reconciles the venues for you. A and C are habits that depended on audited volume; on the constructed bench, ranking by reported volume moved a washed venue from rank 2 to rank 1 at 10 percent wash.

A scientist's three forensic tests fire 0 of 3 on a venue with a washer that copies honest trade sizes at 70 percent. Where does that go on the transfer map?

The copy bot reads inside the honest bands by construction. That is the honest limit of a size-based screen, not a failure and not a clean bill of health. A forensic screen is only learned when its false-positive rate and the case it cannot catch travel with it.

Which item on the 90-day ladder is an official source?

IOSCO is the international securities standard setter and the report is its own publication, though non-binding. The working paper is free and peer-reviewed in its published form, but it is research, not an official source; the sibling courses are free and not official.

Finish the map and date it
