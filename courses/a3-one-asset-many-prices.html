Learn Crypto with Phoebe · Leader session 3 of 6

One asset, many prices

A conventional market feed has one consolidated record of every trade, so "the price at 13:00" is a lookup. A crypto asset trades on many venues at once, each with its own tape, its own book, its own basis and its own clock, and nobody stitches them into one record. At 13:00 there are as many prices as there are venues, and some of them are hours old while looking fresh. Tonight four constructed venues meet four defensible methods and produce four different reference levels, and the leader in the room learns what has to be signed alongside the number. Nothing here is a price of anything real.

Part 0

Harrowgate, and the one number it has to publish

Harrowgate is a constructed data team at a fund-administration firm. Session 1 gave it a boundary and session 2 gave it a word for the volume column. Now the client wants one daily reference level for the abstract asset, the number a valuation is struck at. The team opens the feeds at 13:00 and finds four venues, four last prints, and nothing that says which one is official. None of them is. That is not a gap in the data team's knowledge; it is the shape of the market.

LiveFour prints, none official2 min▶

Harrowgate's first instinct was the one a conventional feed trains: find the primary venue, take its last print, done. There is no primary venue. The four venues trade the same abstract asset with different crowds, different fees and different amounts of activity, and each publishes its own tape. At 13:00 their last prints read 99.670, 99.706, 99.637 and 99.969 on the constructed tape. All four are real prints. They do not agree, and D sits far above the other three.

The second instinct was to average them. That is a method, and it is one of four that a careful person could defend. Each gives a different level, and the difference is not noise to be rounded away: it is the choice of method showing through.

Real world

Harrowgate's analyst put the simple mean in the first draft, 99.7455, and the reviewer asked one question: why is the level higher than three of the four venues? The answer was venue D, whose price had stopped moving at 10:00 and was still being counted as an equal. Harrowgate is constructed; the question is the one a good reviewer always asks.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Venue. One exchange with its own tape, its own book and its own reported volume. Tonight there are four, labelled A to D.
  • Consolidated tape. One record of every trade on every venue, in time order. A conventional feed has one; this market does not.
  • Last print. The most recent trade on one venue. What a venue "says the price is" at a moment.
  • Age. How long ago the last print happened. A quiet venue's last print can be minutes old while a busy one is seconds old.
  • Basis. A persistent gap between one venue's prices and the others', from fees, access or who trades there. Venue B's basis is +0.040; venue C's is -0.030.
  • Window. The stretch of time a method looks back over. Tonight five minutes, 12:55 to 13:00.
  • Reference level. The one number published after reconciling venues. Also called a fixing or an index level.
  • Venue screen. The written rule that decides which venues may contribute at all, before any weighting happens.
Part 1 · covers no consolidated tape

No consolidated tape, so every venue is a separate witness

12 min live

A consolidated tape is a service most data teams never think about. Every trade on every venue lands in one record under one clock, so "the last trade at 13:00" has exactly one answer and everyone downstream reads the same one. Take the consolidation away and each venue becomes a separate witness to the same market, standing at a different distance from it, speaking at a different moment, and sometimes repeating a statement it made three hours ago.

LiveWhat a consolidated tape quietly does4 min▶

On a conventional feed the consolidated record does three jobs nobody lists. It puts every venue's trades in one sequence, so "the last trade" is one row. It stamps them with one clock, so "before 13:00" means the same thing for every venue. And it makes the market's price a lookup rather than a calculation, which is why nobody on a conventional desk is ever asked which method produced the price at 13:00.

Here each venue publishes its own tape with its own timestamps. A data team that wants one price has to collect four records, decide whether their clocks agree, decide how far back to look on each, and then combine them by some rule. Every one of those steps is a decision, and the consolidated tape used to make all of them invisibly. How a stream from four sources gets ordered and how late arrivals are handled is a subject of its own, in learn-streaming-data-with-phoebe.

LiveFour venues at 13:00, read row by row4 min▶

Read the figure as a witness list. Venue A is busy, 3,000 trades a day, with 13 trades and 249.101 units in the last five minutes; its last print is 99.670 and only 12 seconds old. Venue B trades 1,500 a day and carries a basis of +0.040: 4 trades, 207.500 units, last print 99.706, 80 seconds old. Venue C is thin, 600 a day, basis -0.030: 2 trades, 35.653 units, last print 99.637, 73 seconds old. Venue D trades 250 a day and has not traded at all in the window; its last print is 99.969, 392 seconds old, and its price stopped following the market at 10:00.

The constructed market path that all four venues were drawn around sits at 99.665 at that minute. No real index ever sees that line; it exists here so you can check each witness against the truth. A is closest because it is busy and has no basis. B and C are off by roughly their basis. D is far above all of them, and nothing in its own tape says so.

Self-studyA stuck price is not the same as an old print4 min▶

The obvious defence against a bad venue is an age limit: ignore any venue whose last print is too old. It is a good rule and it catches silence. It does not catch venue D. D is still trading, just rarely and at a price that stopped moving at 10:00, so its last print is 392 seconds old, comfortably inside a 600-second limit. A venue with a broken price feed or a frozen book can keep printing for hours and pass every freshness test.

What catches D is a distance rule: drop any quote too far from the others. At 13:00 D sits 0.28 percent from the median of the four, against a tolerance of 0.2 percent, so the distance rule drops it. Both rules are choices, both numbers (600 seconds, 0.2 percent) are set by the team here rather than read from any standard, and a team that uses only one of them has decided which kind of failure it will miss. Practitioner session 3 builds both on the same tape. General outlier detection beyond this one distance rule lives in learn-anomaly-detection-with-phoebe.

Part 2 · covers the four methods and exclusion

Four methods, four levels

15 min live

There are four standard ways to turn several venue quotes into one level, and each is defensible. A simple mean treats every venue as an equal witness. A median takes the middle witness and ignores how far out the others stand. A volume-weighted mean lets each venue speak in proportion to how much it traded. A volume-weighted median finds the price at which half the traded volume sits on either side. Same four quotes, four different weighings, four levels.

LiveFour levels, and which venue moved each one4 min▶

With all four venues in, the simple mean reads 99.7455, the median 99.688, the volume-weighted mean 99.6828 and the volume-weighted median 99.670. The four are 75.5 ticks apart, 0.076 percent, on quotes that did not change. A report that prints one of them without the method has printed a quarter of a fact.

Each method fails in a way you can name. The simple mean is the one D drags: a stuck venue gets the same vote as the busiest one, so the mean sits above three of the four quotes. The median ignores D's distance but also ignores that C barely traded. The volume-weighted mean gives D nothing, because D had no volume in the window, and leans on A and B, which is right if their volume is real. The volume-weighted median lands on A's own last print, because A alone carries the middle of the volume.

LiveSwitch the rules on, and read what was dropped4 min▶

On Tape A, tick the rules box. The age rule lets every venue through, D included, because D's last print is 392 seconds old and the limit is 600. The distance rule drops D, which sits 0.28 percent from the median against a tolerance of 0.2 percent. Now the four methods read 99.671, 99.670, 99.6828 and 99.670. The mean fell back among the others. The volume-weighted mean did not move at all, because D had no volume to weight in the first place.

That last detail is the useful one for a leader. The volume-weighted methods were already protected from D by the fact that D was quiet. They are not protected from a venue that is loud for the wrong reasons, which is Part 3. Try the other tapes too: on most of them the rules drop nothing at 13:00, and the four methods still disagree, because basis and volume differ even when every venue is healthy.

Self-studyWhat one public index rulebook does instead of a last print4 min▶

One public index rulebook, read at source, does not take anyone's last print. It pools the trades of every selected venue over a fixed 3,600-second calculation window, splits the window into 10 partitions of 6 minutes, and takes the volume median trade in each partition, which the rulebook describes as "outlier resistant by nature". The partition values are then combined with time weights from 1/10 to 10/10, so the most recent minutes count most. A partition with no trades is left out and the weights adjusted; if the whole window has no trades, the level is not published at all.

Before any of that, venues are screened quarterly. The criteria include regulation by an independent government body, KYC/AML controls, trading policies, five years of operation, absence from sanction lists and reliable trade feeds, plus a liquidity floor: a venue must carry at least 0.5 percent of observed liquidity in the pair over the past three months. A second methodology is reported to use 12 five-minute partitions, a volume-weighted median in each, and an equal-weighted average of the partitions. Both are long, careful answers to tonight's question, and neither is a last print. Practitioner session 3 builds partitions on the constructed tape.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

One camp holds that volume weighting is the only honest weighing: a venue where more trading happens is where the price is being discovered, and a level that gives a thin venue the same vote as a deep one is pretending the thin venue knows as much. On this view the job is to screen venues hard and then trust their volume.

The other camp holds that any weight a venue can influence will be influenced, and that medians, which ignore how far out a quote stands and cap how much any one venue can pull, are the safer default. On this view volume weighting is a door left open and the screen is a lock that has to be checked every quarter. Both camps agree that the method is a choice and that the choice belongs beside the level; they disagree on which failure is worse.

Part 3 · build-along

What the leader signs

15 min hands on

Session 2 showed a venue washing its volume. Put that venue into tonight's market. With venue B at 60 percent wash on the constructed tape, B carries 45.9 percent of the volume in the five-minute window, against a 24.8 percent share of the day's honest volume (45.2 percent of what the venues report). The volume-weighted mean moves from 99.6828 to 99.6866, toward B and its +0.040 basis, without one more honest trade. The volume-weighted median stays at 99.670, because A still holds the middle of the volume, until a bigger washer tips it. No code tonight: six steps, said out loud, on a level your own team signs.

Name the level you sign

Pick one reference number your team publishes or relies on: a valuation price, a fixing, a blended rate. Write what it is, at what time it is struck, and how many sources feed it. If the answer to the last is "one", ask what happens the day that source goes quiet or goes stuck.

Write the method in one line

Mean, median, volume-weighted mean or volume-weighted median, over what window, in how many partitions. If nobody in the room can say it, the method is whatever the first engineer typed, and on Tape A that choice alone moves the level by 75.5 ticks.

Write the exclusion rule with its numbers

An age limit, a distance from the median, or both, with the actual thresholds. Then test it against a stuck source that keeps printing. On Tape A the 600-second age rule let venue D through and the 0.2 percent distance rule caught it. A rule with only one of the two has chosen which failure it will miss.

Write the venue screen before the weights

Which sources may contribute at all, decided by a written rule and reviewed on a calendar, the way one public index rulebook screens quarterly on regulation, controls, trading policies, years of operation and a liquidity floor. A volume-weighted method without a screen hands the weights to whoever reports the most.

Run the washer through your method

Ask which of your numbers moves if one source inflates its volume. On the washed market the volume-weighted mean moved to 99.6866 and the volume-weighted median held at 99.670. Neither is immune: the median only held because B's inflated volume was still under half. The screen is what keeps the washer out; the method only decides how much it costs you once it is in.

Commit to the publication line

Write the finished level the way it will appear: the number, the method, the window, the venues that contributed, and what was excluded and why. "99.670, volume-weighted median of three screened venues, five-minute window to 13:00, venue D excluded at 0.28 percent from the median." Long, and finished.

Real world

Harrowgate's signed version had three lines under the level, not one. The method: volume-weighted median over a stated window. The exclusion rule: an age limit and a distance limit, both with numbers, and the day's exclusions listed by venue. The screen: the written criteria each contributing venue had passed, with the date of the last review. The client's auditor asked for nothing else, because there was nothing left to ask. Harrowgate is constructed; the three lines are the standard ones.

The habit to keep. When someone hands you "the price" of anything that trades in more than one place, ask three questions before you ask the number: by what method, with what exclusions, from which screened sources. A level without those three is a level somebody else chose for you.

Check yourself

Three questions

On constructed Tape A at 13:00 the same four venue quotes give 99.7455, 99.688, 99.6828 and 99.670. Why four levels?

The quotes never change between methods. What changes is what each quote is allowed to weigh. That is a decision, which is why the method has to be published beside the level; there is no consolidated tape to settle it.

Venue D's price has been stuck since 10:00 and its last print is 392 seconds old. What does it do to a simple mean of the four venues?

A simple mean ignores volume, so D's lack of trading does not protect it. And D is still printing, so its last print is younger than 600 seconds and the age rule lets it through. The distance rule, 0.28 percent from the median against 0.2, is what drops it.

Why does a volume-weighted method need a venue screen?

Volume weighting is right when the volume is real. The rulebooks keep the weights and decide first which venues may contribute at all. Without that, the venue that reports the most decides the most.
