The volume that reports itself

On a conventional feed, volume is the quiet number nobody argues about. An operator counts every trade once, the trades land in one consolidated record, and somebody outside checks the count. On a crypto venue the venue counts its own trades, publishes the total itself, and nobody stands behind it. Then a league table sorts every venue by that number, so the venue that reports more is seen first. Tonight is what that does to a volume table: two published estimates of how much of the reported volume is wash, why they disagree, and a constructed two-venue bench in this page where you can watch a ranked table promote the venue a detector flags. Nothing here is investment advice, and nothing here is a real venue or a real price.

Part 0

Harrowgate, and the venue volume table it has to publish

Harrowgate is a constructed data team at a fund-administration firm. After session 1 it publishes a daily reference level with the boundary written beside it. The second deliverable is a venue volume table: which venues trade the abstract asset, how much each one trades, and therefore how much weight each gets in the reference level. The first draft took an afternoon. It ranks venues by the volume each one publishes about itself, because that is the column every venue provides. Tonight is about why that column is a claim rather than a measurement, and what the finished table looks like instead.

★ What you walk out with tonight. The difference between counted volume and reported volume, drawn as two pipelines rather than asserted. Two published estimates of how much reported volume is wash, 77.5 percent and about 95 percent, with the setups that produced each, and the reason neither is "the" wash rate. A constructed bench in this page where a washed venue's weight climbs from 52.7 to 78.8 percent of the table while its honest share never moves. And a three-question audit for any volume table your team publishes: who counted each number, which column says so, and what the table does with a venue that reports more than it trades.

LiveThe first draft of Harrowgate's table2 min▶

The draft has four columns: venue, reported volume over 24 hours, rank, and weight in the reference level. Rank is reported volume sorted descending. Weight is each venue's reported volume divided by the total. Every step is the step a conventional market-data team would take, and on a conventional feed it would be correct, because the volume column would have been counted by an operator and checked by someone with no stake in it.

Here the volume column was typed in by the venues. Nobody at Harrowgate counted a trade. Nobody outside the venues reconciled anything. And the rank column, which the team thought of as a summary, is the very thing the venues compete on.

Real world

The reviewer who caught the problem did not find a wrong number. She asked one question of each row: "who counted this?" Every answer was the venue itself. That question, asked of every number in a table, is the whole skill tonight. Harrowgate is constructed; the draft is the ordinary one.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Reported volume. The total a venue publishes about its own trading. A claim made by the party being measured. Tonight.
  • Wash trade. A trade in which the same interest sits on both sides, so volume is printed and nothing changes hands in any economic sense. On the bench it is a buy and a sell of the same size a few seconds apart. Practitioner session 6 builds them.
  • Wash share. The part of a venue's reported volume that is wash. Always a share of something, and the something is where estimates part company.
  • League table. A public ranking of venues by reported volume. The thing that turns the volume number into a target.
  • Weight. How much one venue's price counts in a reference level. Weighting by volume is standard practice and correct when the volume is real.
  • Screen. The rule that decides which venues may contribute at all, before any weighting happens. Where the public index rulebooks do their real work.
  • Provenance. Who produced a number, how, and with what stake in it. The column a volume table usually lacks.
  • Denominator. What a percentage is a percentage of. Two wash estimates with different denominators are answers to different questions.
Part 1 · covers who counts the volume

Who counts the volume

12 min live

Volume on a conventional feed is a byproduct of somebody else's job. An operator matches the trades, so it counts them; the trades from every venue land in one consolidated record, so a total is a sum rather than a negotiation; and an auditor stands behind the count with nothing to gain from a bigger one. Take those three away and put a league table where the auditor was, and the same arithmetic produces a number with a completely different meaning.

LiveWhat an audited count quietly comes with4 min▶

Look at the left pipeline as a list of services. The operator counts each trade once, because it matched the trade and has the record. The consolidated record means a market-wide total is a sum that anybody can recompute. And the auditor, or an examiner, or simply a counterparty with its own books, is a second party whose job is to notice if the count is wrong. None of those people is paid more if the number is bigger. That last property is the one a data team never writes down, because on a conventional feed it is never in doubt.

Now the right pipeline. The venue matches its own trades and counts them, which is fine. Then it publishes the total itself, with no consolidated record to reconcile against and no auditor behind it. Then something outside the venue sorts every venue by that total and shows the sorted list to the people deciding where to trade. The count has become the venue's entry in a contest, and the venue is the only party that counts it.

The arithmetic in Harrowgate's draft table is identical on both sides of the figure. The meaning of the volume column is not. On the left it is a measurement. On the right it is a claim made by the party being measured, about the thing it is ranked on.

LiveThe league table is the incentive4 min▶

A wash trade adds a buy and a sell of the same size, seconds apart, with the same interest on both sides. It moves nothing anywhere, it costs the washer close to nothing on a venue it controls, and it adds its size twice to the reported total. On a feed with an auditor, a pattern like that is something a second party would look for. On a feed without one, the only check is whether somebody downstream runs a test.

The loop drawn in marigold is why the number drifts upward rather than sitting still. A higher rank means the venue is listed first, which brings attention and flow, which is what the venue is in business for. So the reported volume is not a neutral measurement that happens to be unaudited. It is a target, and a number that is also a target behaves like one. The published study in Part 2 put a figure on the reward: on the ranking data it used, 70 percent wash in total reported volume moved a venue up 46 positions.

None of this needs anybody to be a villain. It needs only a number that the measured party produces, that nobody checks, and that pays the measured party when it is larger. Any data leader who has run a sales league table built on self-reported pipeline has seen the same shape.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

One camp holds that self-reported volume from an unaudited venue is not data at all. If the measured party produces the number and is paid for a bigger one, no test can restore it to the status of a measurement, and a serious table should use something else: only venues that answer to an independent supervisor, or a quantity the venue cannot inflate cheaply.

The other camp holds that the honest part of the number is still information, that the crude fakes leave fingerprints in the trade sizes, and that a screened, tested, labelled volume column is better than no column. The forensic tests in practitioner sessions 4 to 7 come from this camp, and their honest limit, a washer that copies honest trade sizes and is invisible to every size-based test, is the strongest argument the first camp has.

Both camps agree on the thing that matters tonight: a volume number without its provenance is not finished. The disagreement is about what to do once the provenance is written down, and the person across the table will belong to one camp or the other. General detection methods, beyond the forensic ones specific to a self-reported tape, live in learn-anomaly-detection-with-phoebe.

Part 2 · covers the two estimates and the rank trap

Two estimates, one disagreement

15 min live

Ask how much of the reported volume on crypto venues is wash and two numbers come back. A peer-reviewed academic study estimated 77.5 percent on average across unregulated venues in 2019. An industry presentation the same year reported about 95 percent of one aggregator's figure for one asset. A reader who sees both assumes one is wrong. Neither is. They are answers from two different measuring setups, and the honest move is to keep both, each with its setup written beside it.

LiveSetup 1: the academic estimate, and its own bounds4 min▶

Cong, Li, Tang and Yang, "Crypto Wash Trading", NBER working paper w30783, read at source; the published version appeared in Management Science in 2023 (reported). They studied 29 venues from 9 July to 3 November 2019: 3 regulated, and 26 unregulated, split by web traffic into 10 Tier-1 and 16 Tier-2. They ran three forensic tests on trade sizes, including the first-digit law and clustering at round sizes, and found the direction that surprises people: the regulated venues showed the clustering at round sizes that human traders produce, and many unregulated venues showed too little of it.

Their quantification uses exactly that. A ratio of unrounded to round volume is estimated on the regulated venues; any unrounded volume on an unregulated venue above what that ratio predicts is counted as wash. The result: an average of 77.5 percent of unregulated venues' volume, median 79.1 percent, above 53.4 percent for Tier-1 and 81.7 percent for Tier-2, and about 61 percent even after controlling for venue characteristics. On the ranking data they used, 70 percent wash moved a venue up 46 positions.

Read the bounds the authors put on it themselves. Their first-digit estimates are significantly lower and serve as rough lower bounds. If honest algorithmic trading produces unrounded sizes, the roundness estimate is closer to an upper bound. And venues may have adapted since the 2019 sample. Every figure above is the authors' estimate on their 2019 sample, with those bounds, and it travels with them.

LiveSetup 2: the industry estimate, reported3 min▶

Fusaro and Hougan, an asset manager's presentation to the US securities regulator, March 2019. Reported rather than read at source, because the hosted document is image-only. As reported, it found that about 95 percent of the volume a widely used aggregator showed for one asset was fake or non-economic. It looked at 81 venues over four days and found 10 with real volume, using trade-size histograms, volume patterns and spread behaviour. It is an industry presentation, not peer reviewed.

None of that makes it wrong. It makes it a different setup: a different list of venues, a window of four days rather than four months, a different method, and a percentage of a different total. Cong and his co-authors cite it and describe their own estimates as being in the same order of magnitude as the industry reports.

LiveWhy 77.5 and 95 do not contradict3 min▶

Put the two setups side by side, as in the figure, and the disagreement stops looking like a contradiction. The denominators differ: 77.5 percent is a share of unregulated venues' own volume, with the regulated venues near zero by construction of the method; about 95 percent is a share of one aggregator's total for one asset, across whatever venues it listed. The samples differ: 26 unregulated venues against 81. The windows differ: about four months against four days. The methods differ: a regression benchmarked on regulated venues against histograms and pattern reading.

So the question "which one is the wash rate?" has no answer, because there is no single quantity both are estimating. The professional sentence names both, each with its setup: "a 2019 academic study estimated 77.5 percent of unregulated venues' volume, bounded both ways by its authors; a 2019 industry presentation reported about 95 percent of one aggregator's figure for one asset." Averaging them would produce a third number that describes no setup at all. Picking one would throw away the reason they differ, which is the most useful thing either tells you.

LiveWhat ranking by reported volume does to a washed venue3 min▶

Start on Tape A with no washer. Venue B's honest weight is 52.7 percent of the pair and its weight by reported volume is the same, because every reported unit is a real one. Now add the washer. B's honest weight stays at 52.7 percent on every rung, since the washer adds trades on top of the same honest tape. Its weight by reported volume climbs: 55.3 at 10 percent wash, 61.4 at 30, 69.0 at 50 and 78.8 percent at 70. Every point above 52.7 is wash being counted as if it measured anything.

On Tape A the rank never moves, because B was already the larger honest venue; only the weight does. Switch pairs to see the rank move. On Tapes B and C the washed venue goes from rank 2 to rank 1 at 10 percent wash; on Tapes D and E it takes rank 1 at 30 percent. At 70 percent wash its weight is 76.3, 75.3, 73.5 and 73.1 percent on those four pairs, against honest weights of 49.1, 47.7, 45.4 and 44.9.

Read the last row of the panel too. On every pair, every rung from 10 percent up is flagged by the detectors, three of three, and the honest rung never fires. The table ranked by reported volume promotes exactly the venue the detectors flag. How those detectors work, and their limits, is practitioner sessions 4 to 7.

Self-studyThe honest nuance: weighting is right when the volume is real4 min▶

The tempting lesson is "never weight by volume". It is the wrong lesson. Volume weighting is standard practice in every public index rulebook read here, and it is correct when the volume is real: a venue where more genuine trading happens should count for more in a reference level. On the bench, at 0 percent wash, weighting by reported volume gives exactly the honest weight. The trouble is not the weight. It is weighting a number nobody has checked.

So the rulebooks do their work earlier. One public index rulebook screens venues quarterly on criteria including regulation by an independent government body, KYC/AML controls, trading policies, five years of operation, absence from sanction lists and reliable trade feeds, plus a liquidity floor; its listed exclusion actions include fraud and market manipulation. Only venues that survive the screen contribute, and then their volume is weighted. Screen first, label provenance, then weight what survives. Practitioner session 3 builds the methods; session 3 on this track reconciles venues into a level.

Part 3 · build-along

Audit your own volume table

15 min hands on

No code. Six steps on a table your own team publishes or relies on, said out loud in the room. Any table that ranks, weights or filters sources by a number the sources report about themselves will do: venues, vendors, branches, sales regions. The questions are the same ones Harrowgate's reviewer asked.

Pick a table that sorts by a reported number

A league table, a weighting scheme, a "top ten sources" list. Write down the number it sorts or weights by, and what decision downstream changes when the order changes.

Ask of every row: who counted this?

Three possible answers: we counted it ourselves from records; an operator counted it and someone outside checks; the source counted it about itself. Mark each row. Most teams find the third answer in more rows than they expected.

Find the column that says so

If the table has no column for provenance, a reader cannot tell a measurement from a claim, and neither could you a year from now. The fix is a column, not a footnote: "reported by the venue, unaudited" in the same row as the number.

Follow a venue that reports more than it trades

Pretend one row is 70 percent wash. Trace what the table does with it: its rank, its weight, the attention it gets downstream. On the bench, Tape A's washed venue went from 52.7 to 78.8 percent of the table. If your table would do the same, the table is part of the loop in Part 1.

Write the screen before the weight

What rule decides which sources may contribute at all? Name it in one sentence, the way one public index rulebook names regulation, trading policies, years of operation and a liquidity floor. If there is no rule, every source that publishes a number is in, which is the rule the washer would choose.

Commit to the word

Session 1 promised a word for beside the volume figure, and it is not "audited". It is "reported", with by whom: "24-hour volume as reported by the venue". Write it on the table in front of you. A number with that word beside it can be weighted honestly; a number without it is waiting to be misread.

Real world

Harrowgate's second draft changed three things and no arithmetic. The volume column was renamed "24-hour volume as reported by the venue". A new column said which venues passed the team's written screen, and only those contributed to the reference level. And a third column carried the result of the size tests from the practitioner track, as a flag for review rather than an automatic exclusion, because a test that fires on 1.5 percent of honest venues on the bench is a reason to look, not a verdict. Harrowgate is constructed; the three changes are the standard ones.

The habit to keep. When a table ranks or weights anything by a number, ask who counted it before you ask whether it is large. A count produced by the party being ranked is a claim, and the table is only finished when the claim is labelled as one.

Check yourself

Three questions

A board paper quotes 77.5 percent wash; a colleague's slide says about 95 percent. Why do these not contradict each other?

A · They do: the industry figure is not peer reviewed, so it should be discarded
B · The true wash rate is the average of the two, so the report should quote that
C · They come from different setups: different venues, windows, methods and denominators, so neither is "the" wash rate
77.5 percent is the academic average share of unregulated venues' volume over about four months of 2019; about 95 percent is a reported share of one aggregator's figure for one asset over four days. Different questions, different answers. Averaging them describes no setup, and discarding one throws away the reason they differ.

On the constructed bench, venue B carries a round-lot washer at 70 percent of its reported volume. What does a table ranked and weighted by reported volume do with it?

A · Raises its weight from its honest 52.7 percent to 78.8 percent, while the detectors flag it three of three
B · Nothing, because wash trades buy and sell the same size and cancel out of the volume
C · Lowers its weight, because the detectors remove flagged volume before the ranking
A wash pair adds its size twice to reported volume; it does not cancel. The ranking reads only the reported column, so every unit of wash raises the weight. The detectors flag the venue, but a table that sorts by reported volume never asks them.

What is the honest response for a team that weights venues by volume?

A · Stop weighting by volume and give every venue an equal weight
B · Screen which venues may contribute, label who counted each number, and keep weighting the volume that survives the screen
C · Discount every venue's reported volume by 77.5 percent
Weighting by volume is correct when the volume is real, which is why the public rulebooks keep it and screen venues first. Equal weights give a washed venue the same say as an honest one, and a blanket discount applies a 2019 estimate about a different set of venues to yours.
Before session 3

Homework

Twenty minutes, on the table you audited and on the sentence you wrote.

Add the provenance column

On one real table, add a column that says who counted each number, in plain words. Show it to one person who reads the table and ask whether any row surprised them.

Bring one number that appears in two places

Any figure your team publishes that also appears in another team's report or another source, with a different value. Session 3 is about one asset with many prices at once, and the question it asks is which of the two is "the" number, and why that question has no answer.
