Reading an exchange tape

Put the charts down and start where a data engineer always starts: what is a row, what are the columns, and what is the grain. On an exchange tape the answers are unusual in a specific and learnable way. A row is one executed trade, a print. The columns are a venue's own clock, a side, a size in units and a price in ticks. The book snapshot that sits beside it on every screen is a different object entirely, a list of resting intentions that may never execute. And the candle everybody looks at is not on the tape at all; it is built from prints by a rule somebody chose. Tonight is those four facts, read off Harrowgate's constructed tape, plus the honest admission that 169 of the day's 1,440 minutes contain no print whatsoever.

Part 0

Harrowgate hands you a feed and asks for candles by Friday

Harrowgate is a constructed data team at a fund-administration firm. It has a feed from one venue, a request for five-minute candles and a daily volume table, and a reasonable assumption that this is like every other market feed it has ingested. The first thing to do with any new source is to refuse to look at it as a chart and look at it as a table instead. The tape in this page is generated by the same engine that runs every bench in the course, so every number below is computed here and the structure is declared: 3,000 honest prints a day, sizes drawn to span several orders of magnitude, prices in ticks of an abstract unit, no asset, no venue, no currency.

LiveWhy the table view comes before the chart view3 min▶

A chart of a market feed shows candles. A candle is four prices and a volume over an interval, which means a rule was applied before you saw anything: an interval was chosen, prints were bucketed, four of them were picked out and the sizes were summed. Every one of those steps is a decision, and none of them is visible on the chart. The table view shows what the venue actually published, one executed trade per row, and lets you make the decisions yourself, on purpose, where they can be written down.

The habit transfers from every other event feed a data engineer has handled: clickstreams, payments, sensor logs. The grain is the event. Aggregates are derived and regenerable. The person who ingests at aggregate grain because that is what the dashboard shows has thrown away the only thing that happened and kept a summary of it made by a rule they did not choose.

Real world

Harrowgate's first ingestion job pulled five-minute candles because that was what the venue's chart endpoint returned, and stored them as if they were observations. The second week, somebody asked for one-minute candles. There was no way to make them, because a five-minute bar cannot be split, and the prints that would have allowed it had never been kept. Harrowgate is constructed; the mistake is common.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Print. One executed trade on the tape. Timestamp, side, size, price. The grain of everything.
  • Side. Which side the aggressor was on: a buy means someone lifted an ask, a sell means someone hit a bid. Every trade has a buyer and a seller; the side says who crossed the spread.
  • Size. Quantity traded, in units of the asset. On this tape, an integer number of millionths of a unit, so that every later test is exact arithmetic.
  • Tick. The smallest price step. Prices here are in ticks of 0.001 of the quoting unit; 100,007 ticks reads as 100.007.
  • Book snapshot. The resting bids and asks at a moment, grouped by price level, with the quantity at each. Intent, not execution.
  • Spread. Best ask minus best bid. Ten ticks at noon on the constructed tape.
  • Depth. The number of price levels available in the book at a moment, in a public market-data definition. Eight a side in the snapshot here.
  • Candle, bar. Open, high, low, close and volume over an interval you chose. Practitioner session 2 builds them and shows how much depends on the choice.
Part 1 · covers the three objects

A print is a fact, a book is an intention, a candle is a rule

14 min live

Three things appear on every market screen and they are three different kinds of object. The tape records what executed. The book records what people have offered and not yet withdrawn. The candle is neither: it is what you get when you apply an interval and four selection rules to the tape. Confusing any two of them produces a pipeline that stores the wrong thing with great care.

LiveThe four columns, and what each one is not5 min▶

The clock is the venue's own. It says when the venue's matching engine recorded the fill, not when your collector received it, and two venues' clocks agree with each other only to the extent that two computers' clocks do. The first print on the constructed tape is at second 12 of the day; the venue did not consult you about that.

The side is the aggressor's side, the party who crossed the spread. Every trade has a buyer and a seller, so "buy" does not mean more buyers than sellers; it means the taker lifted a resting ask. A tape with 50 percent buys is not a balanced market, it is a tape where the aggressors were evenly split, which is a different and much weaker statement.

The size is a quantity of the asset. On this tape it is stored as an integer number of millionths of a unit so that the forensic tests in sessions 4 and 5 are exact arithmetic rather than floating point; 0.223 units is the integer 223,000. A conventional feed usually gives you a float, and the first thing to decide is the precision you will keep.

The price is in ticks of the quoting unit. 99,998 ticks reads as 99.998. The quoting unit is not the asset and, on this feed, may itself be a traded thing; session 8 is what happens when it moves. Nothing on the tape tells you that, which is why the restatement in session 8 has to come from a second source.

LiveThe book is a different object, and it is not on the tape4 min▶

At noon the constructed book shows 33.3 units bid at 100.002 and 6 units offered at 100.012, a spread of ten ticks, with eight price levels on each side. None of those quantities traded. They are resting orders, and every one of them can be cancelled a millisecond after the snapshot without leaving a trace on the tape. A public market-data definition calls the number of price levels at a moment the book's depth, and the difference between the highest bid and the lowest ask the spread. Both are statements about intent.

The practical consequence is that a book snapshot and a tape row cannot be joined as if they were the same grain. A snapshot is a moment; a print is an event. A pipeline that stores "the book" stores a series of moments it chose, and the choice of sampling rate is as consequential as the choice of candle interval. On the constructed tape the snapshot is generated from the tape's seed and the clock as a drawing of what a book looks like; it is not a matching engine, and the page says so.

Self-studyThe two tables you would actually keep5 min▶

Prints, at print grain. One row per executed trade: venue, venue timestamp, receipt timestamp, side, size as an integer in a stated precision, price as an integer in ticks, and the venue's own trade identifier if it publishes one. Append only. This is the observation and it is the only table that cannot be regenerated.

Bars, at a stated interval, derived. One row per interval per venue: open, high, low, close, volume, count of prints, and a flag for an interval with no prints at all. Regenerated from the prints table whenever the rule changes. The rule itself, including what an empty interval prints, lives in version control beside the job, not in somebody's head.

Book snapshots, if you keep them, are a third table at a stated sampling rate with the rate in the table name, so that nobody joins a one-second snapshot series to a one-minute one and calls the result depth. The mechanics of windowing an event stream, late events and watermarks are the same as for any other feed and live in the streaming course rather than here.

Part 2 · covers the clock and the gaps

The clock, the gaps, and the minutes with nothing in them

13 min live

A tape is a sequence of events on a venue's clock, and the events are not evenly spaced. The first five prints on the constructed tape arrive at seconds 12, 33, 45, 59 and 70. Over the day, 3,000 prints land in 1,440 minutes, and 169 of those minutes contain none at all. What a pipeline does with an empty minute is a decision, and it is the first decision a candle builder has to make before any candle exists.

LiveA print is the only thing that happened4 min▶

Read the first rows of Tape A. Second 12, a buy of 0.223 units at 99.998. Second 33, a sell of 0.3 at 99.995. Second 45, a sell of 3 at 99.996. That is the whole of what the venue is telling you. There is no "price at second 20"; nothing happened at second 20. There is no "volume at 00:01"; there is the sum of the sizes of whichever prints you decided belong to 00:01, under a bucketing rule that has to say whether a print at exactly 60.000 seconds belongs to the first minute or the second.

The profile under the table is computed the same way. 3,000 prints, 989 distinct sizes, a last print of 99.957, a range of 99.934 to 100.065. None of those numbers required a decision except the last two, which required deciding that the day is the 86,400 seconds the tape covers. Everything a chart adds on top of this table is a decision, and the table is where the decisions should be made visible.

LiveThe empty minute4 min▶

169 of the day's 1,440 minutes contain no print. A one-minute candle series therefore has 169 rows that cannot be observed. The three honest options are to carry the previous close forward and flag the row, to print no row at all, or to print a row of nulls. The dishonest option, and the default in more charting tools than you would expect, is to carry the previous close forward silently, so that a reader sees 1,440 closes and assumes 1,440 trades.

The choice propagates. A volatility computed over carried-forward closes is lower than one computed over observed closes, because 169 of its returns are exactly zero by construction. A count of up and down bars changes, because a carried bar is flat. The count on the constructed tape is 401 up, 375 down and 495 flat at one minute, and the 495 is mostly bars with one print, which open and close on the same price. Practitioner session 2 moves the interval and watches those counts move with it.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

One camp keeps the venue's timestamp as the one true time of a print, on the grounds that it is the time the venue's matching engine recorded the fill, and anything else is a fact about your network. On this view the receipt time is logged for monitoring and never used in a candle.

The other camp points out that the venue's clock is a clock you cannot audit, that two venues' clocks disagree, and that for anything cross-venue the only clock all the sources share is yours. On this view every print carries both timestamps, candles are built on the receipt time with the venue time kept as an attribute, and the disagreement between the two is itself a monitored quantity.

Both camps keep both columns. The disagreement is about which one the bar builder reads, and it matters most in practitioner session 3, where four venues have to be lined up at the same moment and "the same moment" has to be defined in somebody's clock.

Part 3 · build-along

Profile a tape before you build anything on it

15 min hands on

Six steps with pandas on any tape you have as a CSV with the four columns: t in seconds, side, size, px. The widget above prints the constructed tape's answers; your numbers will differ on your own tape, which is the point. Run the code in a scratch folder, never where the outputs could land in a repository.

Load at print grain and assert it

One row per print. If two rows share a venue trade id you have a duplicate; if the timestamp column has a float, decide the precision now.

python · grain checkimport pandas as pd tape = pd.read_csv("tape.csv") # columns: t, side, size, px assert tape["t"].is_monotonic_increasing # the venue's clock, sorted print(len(tape), "prints") print(tape["size"].nunique(), "distinct sizes") print(tape.head(5)) # the first five prints, as published

Keep size and price as integers in a stated precision

Decide the precision once, convert once, and never carry a float through a sum.

python · fixed precisionMICRO = 1_000_000 # millionths of a unit tape["size_u"] = (tape["size"] * MICRO).round().astype("int64") tape["px_t"] = tape["px"].astype("int64") # already in ticks on this tape print(tape["size_u"].sum() / MICRO, "units traded")

Count the empty minutes before building any candle

The number of minutes with no print is the first thing a bar builder needs to know, and it is a property of the tape, not of the bar rule.

python · empty minutesminute = tape["t"] // 60 present = minute.nunique() print(present, "minutes with a print,", 1440 - present, "empty")

Build one five-minute bar by hand and compare with a chart

Open is the first print, close the last, high the max, low the min, volume the sum. Then look at the venue's chart for the same bar and see whether it agrees. If it does not, the venue's rule differs from yours and you have just learned something you could not have learned from the chart.

python · one bar, by the rulebar = tape[(tape["t"] >= 33300) & (tape["t"] < 33600)] # 09:15 to 09:20 ohlcv = dict(open=bar["px_t"].iloc[0], high=bar["px_t"].max(), low=bar["px_t"].min(), close=bar["px_t"].iloc[-1], volume=bar["size_u"].sum() / MICRO, prints=len(bar)) print(ohlcv)

Write the empty-bar rule down beside the job

Carry forward with a flag, print nothing, or print nulls. Put the choice in the bar table's name or a column, not in a comment.

python · the flagbars = tape.groupby(tape["t"] // 300)["px_t"].agg(["first", "max", "min", "last", "count"]) bars = bars.reindex(range(288)) # every five-minute slot in the day bars["observed"] = bars["count"].notna() # False on a slot with no print bars["last"] = bars["last"].ffill() # carried forward, and SAID so print((~bars["observed"]).sum(), "carried bars")

Keep the book apart from the tape

If you keep snapshots, put the sampling rate in the table name and never join a snapshot to a print as if they shared a grain. A snapshot is a moment; a print is an event.

python · two grains, two tablesprints.to_parquet("prints.parquet") # the observation book_1s.to_parquet("book_snapshot_1s.parquet") # a moment, sampled every second, said so

The habit to keep. When a feed arrives as candles, ask for the prints. When a dashboard shows depth, ask for the sampling rate. When a number looks observed, ask which rule built it. The tape is the only thing that happened, and everything else is a decision somebody should be able to state.

Check yourself

Three questions

Which of these is an observation rather than a derived object?

A print is one executed trade as the venue published it. A candle close is the last print inside an interval somebody chose, and a VWAP is a weighted sum over prints under a rule. Both can be regenerated from the tape; the print cannot be regenerated from anything.

The noon book snapshot shows 33.3 units bid at 100.002. What does that tell you happened?

A book snapshot is a list of orders that had not executed at that moment. The tape records executions. The last print at noon was 100.007, and the best bid sat five ticks below it; neither is "the price", and the bid is not a trade.

169 of 1,440 minutes contain no print. What is the right handling in a one-minute candle series?

An empty minute is a decision. Carrying forward with a flag keeps the series aligned to the clock and keeps the reader informed. Interpolation invents prices that never printed, and renumbering breaks the alignment that every join and every comparison depends on.
