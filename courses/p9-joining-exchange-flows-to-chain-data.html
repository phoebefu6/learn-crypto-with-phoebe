Joining exchange flows to chain data

Sooner or later somebody asks whether a venue's reported volume squares with what moved in and out of it on the chain. It sounds like a join. It is a join with no key. The venue knows which account owns which deposit address and never publishes it; the tape has no account column at all; the chain has addresses and no names. The only bridge is a label table that a third party compiled and published, with its own sources, its own age and its own mistakes. Tonight you join through that bridge on Harrowgate's constructed tables, measure how much of the flow actually lands and how much of what lands is contested, and learn what a flow cross-check can prove: when it agrees, very little; when it disagrees, that something is wrong, though not yet what.

Part 0

Harrowgate is asked to check the volume table against the chain

Harrowgate is a constructed data team at a fund-administration firm. It publishes a venue volume table, and after session 7 it knows that the tape's own sizes can only take a detector so far: a washer that copies honest sizes is invisible to every size test. So a reviewer asks for a second, independent line of evidence. Wash trades are pairs that start and end inside one venue, and nothing about them needs a transfer on the chain. Real trading, over time, does pull deposits in and send withdrawals out. If a venue's reported volume is mostly real, its flows on the chain should keep some kind of pace with it. The request lands as one line: join venue flows to chain data, compare against reported volume, report. Every table in this page is constructed and printed in full; nothing describes a real venue, address or chain.

LiveWhy the obvious join is the dangerous one3 min▶

Every join a data engineer has written before had a key both sides agreed on: a customer id, an order number, a timestamp and a symbol. Two tables that share a key can still disagree, but at least they disagree about the same thing. Here the two sides describe different things in different vocabularies. The venue's tape speaks in prints and, internally, in accounts. The chain speaks in addresses and transfers. Nothing on either side names anything on the other.

So the join is really two joins: flows to a label table on the address, and the label table to a venue on the name. The second hop is cheap. The first is where every error enters, because the label table is somebody else's opinion about who controls an address, written down at some point in the past, for reasons you may or may not be able to see.

Real world

Harrowgate's first attempt joined the day's flows to the most recent label list it could find, summed by venue, and divided by reported volume. Venue B came out well below venue A, and a draft note began "B's volume appears inflated". Nobody had asked how much of B's flow the label list covered. The answer, worked through tonight, is that the whole gap came from the labels. Harrowgate is constructed; reading a coverage gap as a finding about the venue is a common way to accuse the wrong thing.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Account. The venue's own record of a customer. Known to the venue, never on the public tape.
  • Address. An identifier on the chain that transfers go to and come from. Public, and on its own it says nothing about who controls it.
  • Label. A published claim that an address belongs to a named venue. It has a publisher, a date and a basis: the venue said so, someone observed a deposit, or someone inferred it from a transfer.
  • Label list. A table of labels from one publisher. Two lists can share labels because one copied the other, which makes their agreement worth less than it looks.
  • Gross flow. Inflow plus outflow at an address over the window, in units of the asset. On the constructed day, 10,850 units across 12 addresses.
  • Coverage. The share of gross flow that lands on a usable label under a stated policy.
  • Mismatch. The share of labelled flow where two lists name different venues for the same address.
  • Flow ratio. Gross flow joined to a venue per 100 units of the venue's reported volume. A consistency test, not a measure of anything on its own.
Part 1 · covers the missing join key

The join key that does not exist

14 min live

Put the two sources side by side and look for a column they share. The venue side has a public tape with four columns, clock, side, size and price, and a private ledger that maps its accounts to the addresses it uses. The chain side has addresses and transfers. The only thing that touches both is a table someone else published, mapping some addresses to some venue names. That table is the bridge, and it is the weakest thing in the picture.

LiveTwo ledgers, no shared column5 min▶

Read Figure A left to right. On the venue's side there are two tables. The public tape is the one sessions 1 to 8 have been reading: Tape A's first prints at second 12 and second 33, four columns, and no account column. That is not an omission that a better feed would fix. A venue does not publish who traded, and the tape is anonymous by design. The second table, the ledger that says which of the venue's accounts deposit to which address, exists and is never published at all.

On the chain side, every transfer is public, and the twelve constructed addresses each have an inflow and an outflow for the day. None of them carries a name. Grouping addresses that are probably controlled by one party is a discipline of its own with its own error bounds, and it lives in learn-web3-with-phoebe, practitioner session 3, "Addresses are not people". Tonight takes the chain side as given and asks only what happens when you attach venue names to it.

So the join is flows to labels on the address, then labels to a venue on the name. The second hop is a string match. The first hop carries every assumption in the result.

LiveWhat the venue knows that you do not5 min▶

The venue can answer the question exactly. It knows every deposit address it has handed out, which ones it has retired, which ones it moved to last quarter, and which large transfers were its own housekeeping between its own addresses rather than anybody's trading. None of that is available to Harrowgate, and none of it will be, because a venue that publishes its full address list publishes a map of its customers' money.

That asymmetry decides what kind of evidence a flow cross-check can ever be. It is an outsider's consistency test on a self-reported number, built through a third party's partial and dated opinion of the venue's addresses. It can make a self-reported volume look more or less plausible. It cannot reconcile, because the ledger it would reconcile against is the one you cannot see.

Address a12 in Figure A is the everyday case. Venue B started using it after the older label list was published, and the newer list never picked it up. Its flow is real, it is B's, and no join on the published labels will ever give it to B.

Self-studyWhere the chain-side table comes from4 min▶

The flow table in this page is constructed: one row per address for one day, an inflow and an outflow in units of the asset. A real one is built from chain records by an indexer, and it has properties a market-data engineer is not used to. Recent rows can be replaced after the fact, so a flow table built too close to the head of the chain can change under you, and a join that ran on Monday can stop reproducing on Tuesday. How that happens and how a warehouse should be built to survive it is learn-web3-with-phoebe, practitioner session 6, "Reorgs hit your warehouse".

The mechanics underneath, what a chain is and how a transfer becomes final, are in learn-blockchain-with-phoebe and are a prerequisite for doing this for real rather than something re-taught here. For tonight the only rule needed is the one from session 8, carried across: store which version of the chain-side table your join used, or the join cannot be redone.

Part 2 · covers labels, coverage and mismatch

Labels are reported, not verified

13 min live

Harrowgate found two published label lists. List 1 is 420 days old and has seven labels. List 2 is 30 days old and also has seven, but four of them say plainly that they were copied from list 1. Every label carries a basis: the venue disclosed it, someone observed a deposit, or someone inferred it from a transfer. The widget joins the constructed flow table through these lists under a policy you choose, then runs the cross-check against the reported volume that the engine generates for venue A and its partner venue B.

LiveRead the join at the default4 min▶

Start with either list, conflicts set aside, and no washer. The flow table has 12 addresses and 10,850 units of gross flow. Read the status column. Eight addresses join to a usable label, carrying 75.1 percent of the gross flow. Three have no label on either list: a05 and a12 are venue addresses nobody labelled, and a11 is not a venue at all, so for a11 "no label" is the right answer. Coverage under 100 percent is therefore not automatically a defect. It is a number you report, because you cannot tell from the join which of the unlabelled addresses should have had a label.

Then the mismatch line: 10.4 percent of the labelled flow sits on an address where the two lists name different venues. That is a07, and setting it aside is a decision the policy states. Mismatch is the one error a join can see for itself.

Then the line only constructed data can print. Of the flow that joined, 5.5 percent is joined to the wrong venue: a10, a private address that both lists call venue A. List 1 inferred it from a transfer; list 2 copied list 1. No real join can compute this line, because a real join has no declared truth column. It is here so you can see that the error a join cannot measure is not zero, and that it sits exactly where the lists agree.

LiveChange the policy and watch the error move4 min▶

Switch to list 2 only, the newer list. Coverage is 74.7 percent, but the share of joined flow on the wrong venue jumps to 17.3 percent, because list 2 adds its own inferred label for a07 and gets it wrong as well as carrying the copied a10. Newer is not better when the new labels are inferred.

Now switch to only where both lists agree. It feels like the strict, careful policy. Coverage falls to 56.7 percent, and the wrong-venue share rises to 7.3 percent, against 5.5 under either list. Requiring agreement throws away every correct label that only one list had observed, and keeps the wrong one that agrees because it was copied. Agreement between sources that are not independent is not corroboration; it is one source counted twice.

The professional norm follows directly: read the basis column before the venue column. A label from a disclosure or an observed deposit is evidence. A label inferred from a transfer is a hypothesis. A copied label adds nothing to the label it copied, and a policy that counts it as a second vote is weighting one publisher twice.

Self-studyOne venue, many addresses, and why you do not borrow a fix4 min▶

A venue uses many addresses and changes them over time, which is why a label list goes stale even when every label in it was right on the day it was published. List 1 is 420 days old and has never heard of a12. The tempting fix is to grow the label set by grouping unlabelled addresses with labelled ones that seem to be controlled by the same party. That is address clustering, and it brings its own error bounds that you would then be joining through. How a clustering is built, and how to report one you cannot validate, is in learn-web3-with-phoebe session p3 and session p4. Use its result as it reports it, with its bounds; do not re-derive it inside a market-data job.

The deeper point is that a venue label is a reported attribute of an address, not a resolved identity. Deciding that two records refer to the same real-world entity is entity resolution, with its own vocabulary of match evidence and clerical review, taught in learn-entity-resolution-with-phoebe. A published label list has done some version of that work out of your sight, which is exactly why you keep its publisher, date and basis on every joined row.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

One camp joins only through labels with direct evidence, a venue's own disclosure or an observed deposit, and reports low coverage honestly. On this view an inferred label is a lead to investigate, never an input to a published number, and a flow ratio built on 57 percent of the flow is better than one built on 90 percent of guesses.

The other camp points out that a cross-check with low coverage is nearly useless, because the unlabelled flow dominates every ratio, and that inferred labels and clustering are how the field gets coverage at all. On this view you use the broad label set, carry the basis on every row, and report the result two ways, with and without the inferred labels, so a reader sees how much rests on them.

Both camps keep the basis and the publication date per row, and both report coverage. The disagreement is about which policy produces the headline number, and the widget shows why it matters: on these tables the policy moves the wrong-venue share between 5.5 and 17.3 percent.

Part 3 · build-along

What a cross-check can prove, built in pandas

15 min hands on

The cross-check compares two things that should move together if a venue's volume is real: gross flow joined to the venue, and the venue's reported volume. A wash pair is a buy and a sell between accounts on the same venue, and it needs no transfer anywhere, so a washer raises reported volume and leaves the flow untouched. That is the whole idea. The figure below draws it, and also draws what the test cannot tell apart.

LiveAgreement is weak, disagreement is strong5 min▶

Go back to the widget, keep either-list labels, and move venue B's wash share. With the declared-truth labels, which only a constructed world has, venue B reads 5.00 flow per 100 units with no washer, 3.50 at 30 percent wash and 2.50 at 50 percent, against A's 5.00 throughout. The washer pulls B's ratio down in proportion, exactly as Figure C says it should, because every wash unit is reported volume with no flow behind it.

Now read the published-label row at zero wash: B at 3.04 against A at 5.23, so B reads 0.58 of A with no washer at all. With perfect labels, a washer at 30 percent only takes B to 0.70 of A. The label table alone makes an honest venue look worse than a 30 percent washer. And A's 5.23 is above its true 5.00 because a10, a private address, was counted as A's. A's ratio "agrees" with expectations partly by accident.

That is the asymmetry. When the ratios agree, many different worlds fit: real volume with good labels, or a washer who also cycles coins through their own deposit addresses to manufacture flow, or a wrong label adding exactly the flow that was missing. Agreement rules out very little, so it is weak evidence that the volume is real. When they disagree, the world in which the volume is real, the labels are complete and the venues turn over alike is ruled out. That is strong evidence that something is wrong. It is not evidence of what, and the first two things to check are the two you can measure: coverage and mismatch.

Real world

Cong, Li, Tang and Yang, "Crypto Wash Trading" (NBER working paper w30783, read at source), estimated wash trading from the tape's trade records alone, with three tests on trade sizes, and stated the results with explicit bounds: the Benford-based estimates as "somewhat lower bounds", and, if authentic algorithmic trading inflates unrounded volume, their estimates as closer to upper bounds. They write that "we cannot assert that our estimates are the gold standard". A flow cross-check is a second, independent line of evidence beside tests like theirs. It deserves the same treatment: a stated method, a stated coverage and a stated direction of error.

Six steps with pandas, on three CSVs: labels.csv with one row per address per list (address, list, venue, basis, published_days_ago), flows.csv with one row per address (address, inflow, outflow), and volume.csv with each venue's reported volume in units. Built from the constructed tables in the widget, every printed value below is exact and matches the widget at its default; nothing in these tables is random. Run the code in a scratch folder, never where the outputs could land in a repository.

Load both sides and assert their grain

One row per address in the flow table, one row per address per list in the label table. The flow table came from the chain side at some version; write that version down before anything else.

python · two tables, two grainsimport pandas as pd labels = pd.read_csv("labels.csv") # address, list, venue, basis, published_days_ago flows = pd.read_csv("flows.csv") # address, inflow, outflow (units, one day, chain side) assert flows["address"].is_unique assert not labels.duplicated(["address", "list"]).any() flows["gross"] = flows["inflow"] + flows["outflow"] print(len(flows), "addresses,", flows["gross"].sum(), "units of gross flow") # 12, 10850

Put the lists side by side and find what they disagree on and what they copied

One row per address with each list's venue in its own column. A conflict is two lists naming different venues. A copy is a label whose basis says it came from another list, and it is not a second witness.

python · conflicts and copieswide = labels.pivot(index="address", columns="list", values="venue") # list_1, list_2 both = wide["list_1"].notna() & wide["list_2"].notna() wide["conflict"] = both & (wide["list_1"] != wide["list_2"]) copies = labels["basis"].str.startswith("copied") print(int(wide["conflict"].sum()), "address where the lists disagree") # 1, a07 print(int(copies.sum()), "labels that are copies of another list") # 4

State the policy, then left-join so no flow disappears

The policy is a decision, written in the code before the join runs. A left join keeps every flow row exactly once; an inner join would drop the unlabelled flow and make coverage look like 100 percent.

python · the joinPOLICY = "either list, conflicts set aside" # written down, not discovered wide["venue"] = wide["list_1"].fillna(wide["list_2"]).where(~wide["conflict"]) j = flows.merge(wide[["venue", "conflict"]], left_on="address", right_index=True, how="left", validate="one_to_one") j["conflict"] = j["conflict"].eq(True) # unlabelled rows are not conflicts assert len(j) == len(flows) # every flow, once

Compute coverage and mismatch, weighted by flow

Weight by gross flow, not by address count: one busy unlabelled address matters more than five quiet ones. Print the address count beside it so a reader sees both.

python · coverage and mismatchlabelled = j["address"].isin(wide.index) # some list names it joined = j["venue"].notna() # a usable label landed coverage = j.loc[joined, "gross"].sum() / j["gross"].sum() mismatch = j.loc[j["conflict"], "gross"].sum() / j.loc[labelled, "gross"].sum() print(f"coverage {coverage:.1%} of gross flow, {joined.sum()} of {len(j)} addresses") # 75.1%, 8 of 12 print(f"mismatch {mismatch:.1%} of labelled flow carries conflicting labels") # 10.4%

Run the cross-check against reported volume

Gross flow joined to each venue per 100 units of that venue's reported volume. Compare venues to each other, never to a magic number: there is no published "right" ratio, and the constructed tables make the two venues turn over alike by declaration.

python · flow per 100 unitsvol = pd.read_csv("volume.csv").set_index("venue")["reported_units"] # each venue's own report per_venue = j.groupby("venue")["gross"].sum() ratio = 100 * per_venue / vol print(ratio.round(2)) # A 5.23, B 3.04 print(round(ratio["B"] / ratio["A"], 2), "B against A") # 0.58, with no washer at all

Write the caveat line a reviewer should demand

Generated from the numbers, so it cannot drift from them, and printed beside every ratio the job publishes. It names the labels, the policy, the coverage, the mismatch, and what a low ratio can and cannot mean.

python · the caveatcaveat = (f"Flows joined to venues through two published label lists ({POLICY}). " f"{coverage:.1%} of gross flow landed on a usable label; {mismatch:.1%} of labelled " f"flow carries conflicting labels; copied labels were not counted as confirmation. " f"A low ratio says the volume or the labels are wrong, not which.") print(caveat)

The habit to keep. When two sources share no key, find the table that bridges them and ask who published it, when, and on what basis. When a join lands, report how much did not. When two label sources agree, ask whether one copied the other. When a cross-check agrees, say how little that rules out; when it disagrees, check coverage and mismatch before you name a venue.

Check yourself

Three questions

Why can Harrowgate not join a venue's tape to chain records on a shared key?

A · It can, on the timestamp: a print and a transfer at the same second are the same event
B · Neither side carries the other's identifier: accounts stay in the venue's private ledger, the tape has no account column, and the chain has addresses with no names, so the only bridge is a published label
C · It can, on the size: a trade of 50 units and a transfer of 50 units belong together
A trade on a venue moves nothing on the chain by itself, so neither time nor size links a print to a transfer. The venue knows which addresses are its own and does not publish that. Every joined row passes through a third party's label, and inherits its errors.

Requiring both label lists to agree lowers coverage from 75.1 to 56.7 percent, and the share of joined flow on the wrong venue rises from 5.5 to 7.3 percent. Why does the stricter policy get worse?

A · The stricter policy has fewer rows, so its percentages are noisier
B · List 2 is newer, so requiring list 1 as well adds stale labels
C · The wrong label for a10 is on both lists because list 2 copied it, so agreement keeps it, while correct labels that only one list observed are dropped
Agreement between sources counts as corroboration only when the sources are independent. Four of list 2's seven labels are copies of list 1, including the inferred and wrong a10. Requiring agreement keeps the copied error and discards a03, a04, a08 and a09, all correct.

With either-list labels and no washer on venue B, B reads 3.04 flow per 100 units of reported volume against A's 5.23. What should the reviewer conclude?

A · Something is wrong, either the volume or the labels, and coverage and mismatch must be checked before anyone names the venue; here the whole gap is the label table
B · Venue B's reported volume is inflated by roughly 40 percent
C · Nothing, because a ratio below A's is normal for a smaller venue
A disagreement rules out the world where the volume is real, the labels are complete and the venues turn over alike, but it does not say which of the three failed. On the constructed tables B has no washer; a12 is unlabelled, a07 is set aside and a10 is wrongly counted for A. With the declared-truth labels both venues read 5.00.
Before session 10

Homework

About forty minutes, on any join your team runs through a mapping table somebody else maintains.

Find one bridged join and read its bridge

Any job that joins two sources through a third table: a product mapping, a merchant category list, a vendor-supplied company match. For the bridge table, write down who publishes it, when it was last updated, and whether each row says how it was established. If no row does, that is the finding.

Compute coverage and write the caveat

Re-run the join as a left join and compute the share of the left side, weighted by whatever the downstream number sums, that landed on the bridge. Then write the one-line caveat from step 6 for it. Joining exchange flows to chain data · pin this

No key. The tape has no accounts, the chain has no names, the venue keeps its address list. Every joined row goes through a published label.
A label is reported. Keep publisher, date and basis on every joined row. Disclosure and observed deposits are evidence; inferred labels are hypotheses.
Copies are not votes. Two lists that agree because one copied the other are one witness. "Both must agree" can raise the error.
Left join, always. An inner join drops the unlabelled flow and reports 100 percent coverage of what is left.
Coverage. Share of gross flow on a usable label, flow-weighted, with the address count beside it. 75.1 percent and 8 of 12 on the constructed tables.
Mismatch. Share of labelled flow where lists name different venues. The only label error a join can see; 10.4 percent here.
The cross-check. Flow per 100 units of reported volume, venue against venue. A washer lowers it; so does a missing label.
The asymmetry. Agreement is weak evidence. Disagreement is strong evidence that something is wrong, not of what. Check coverage first.
