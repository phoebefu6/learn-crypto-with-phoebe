Round sizes and clustered sizes

Ask a person how much they want to trade and they say ten, or two, or twenty. Ask a program and it says whatever its arithmetic produced. That difference leaves a mark in the one column a venue cannot avoid publishing, the trade size. Tonight is two tests that read the mark: the share of trades whose size is a round number, and the share that falls on the five most common sizes. Both are calibrated on honest venues, both run in the page, and both carry a twist the published evidence forced on them: the anomaly can go either way. A naive bot is too round. A careful one is not round enough, and that second direction is the one researchers actually found on unregulated venues.

Part 0

Harrowgate learns that people type round numbers

Harrowgate is a constructed data team at a fund-administration firm, and session 4 left it with an uncomfortable result: the first-digit test was the last of three to notice a naive wash bot. The two tests that noticed first read the sizes differently. They do not ask which digit a size starts with; they ask whether the size looks typed by a person. People pick round amounts, and they pick the same few round amounts again and again. A crowd of traders therefore leaves piles on 1, 2, 10 and 20 units with a spread of untidy sizes between them. A bot leaves either far bigger piles or almost none. The tape on this page comes from the same seeded engine as every bench before it: 3,000 honest trades a day, sizes stored as integer micro-units, and a wash bot you can plant in three styles.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A round flag computed with integer arithmetic, and the round share it gives on Harrowgate's constructed Tape A: 29.0 percent. The five most common sizes, 10, 2, 20, 3 and 1 units, holding 11.5 percent of trades. What the published study found about roundness on unregulated venues, read at source, and why it points the opposite way from a naive bot. A band measured on 40 honest venues, 28.3 to 31.6 percent round and 10.8 to 13.5 percent clustered, with two sides. And the honest limit: a bot that copies the crowd's sizes stays inside both bands at 70 percent wash.

LiveA habit a crowd cannot hide3 min▶

A size typed by a person carries the person's habits. Nobody types 7.384 units unless a screen suggested it; people type 7, or 5, or 10. Some trade in tenths, some in whole units, some in round tens, and the crowd as a whole leaves a recognisable mixture of round and untidy sizes. A program has no such habit unless somebody wrote one in. If its author wrote in a list of round lots, it produces round sizes far more often than people do. If its author drew sizes from a random number generator, it produces round sizes far less often than people do.

Either way the venue publishes the evidence itself, in the size column. As in session 4, these tests need nothing from the venue except what it already sends, and as in session 4 they are a screen, not a verdict: they can say the sizes look typed by a crowd or not, and nothing about who traded.

Real world

Harrowgate's analyst took session 4's homework literally and printed the five most common sizes on two venues side by side. The trusted venue's list was 10, 2, 20, 3 and 1 units. The other venue's list was a set of long, untidy numbers nobody would type, each one appearing only a handful of times. Nobody had a test for "too untidy" yet. Harrowgate is constructed; the observation is the one the published study turned into a method.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Round size. On this bench, a size with one significant digit once trailing zeros are stripped: 10, 2, 0.3 and 500 units are round; 2.5, 12 and 7.384 are not.
  • Round share. The share of trades whose size is round. Tape A: 29.0 percent.
  • Cluster share. The share of trades whose size is one of the five most frequent sizes on that tape. Tape A: 11.5 percent.
  • Distinct sizes. How many different sizes appear at all. Tape A has 989 distinct sizes in 3,000 trades.
  • Two-sided band. A range around the honest mean with a floor and a ceiling; a reading outside either side fires.
  • Round-lot bot, flat bot, copy bot. Three planted wash styles. Round lots cycle seven sizes, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50 and 100 units. Flat draws unrounded four-digit sizes from one band, 2 to 20 units. Copy samples the honest size distribution. Session 6 builds all three.
  • Calibration. Measuring the honest spread of a reading on venues you trust before judging a venue you do not, as session 4 did for the MAD.
Part 1 · covers roundness

Roundness: what people type, and what bots do instead

14 min live

The first test is one line of arithmetic per trade: is this size round? Count the trades where the answer is yes and divide by all trades. On Harrowgate's honest Tape A, 29.0 percent of the 3,000 sizes are round. That number only means something next to two others: what honest venues read, and what the published study found on real ones.

LiveOne significant digit, counted with integers5 min▶

Harrowgate stores sizes as integer micro-units, so 10 units is the integer 10,000,000 and 0.3 units is 300,000. The round flag strips trailing zeros and asks whether one digit is left. 10,000,000 strips to 1: round. 300,000 strips to 3: round. 2,500,000 strips to 25: not round. 7,384,000 strips to 7,384: not round. No division by a float, no tolerance, no "close enough to 10". In floating point, 0.1 + 0.2 comes out as 0.30000000000000004, which has far more than one significant digit, and a float-based test would quietly stop working.

Run it over Tape A and 29.0 percent of the 3,000 sizes come back round. The engine that made Tape A rounds a quarter of its simulated traders to one significant digit and the rest to two or three, and some of those also land on a round value by accident, so the measured share sits a few points above the share of round-typers. That is the general shape of the evidence: a round share is a property of the crowd's habits, not of any single trader.

The definition is a choice and the page says so. The published study counted trades in windows around multiples of 100 and 500 base units of each pair, a rule tied to how each market quotes its sizes. The engine uses the simpler one-significant-digit rule, because its sizes are abstract units with no base unit to anchor a window. A team adopting either rule should write down which one it chose, beside the job, and never compare a round share computed one way with a band calibrated the other way.

LiveWhat the published study found, and in which direction5 min▶

The intuitive story is that a wash bot trades in round lots, so a venue with too many round trades is suspect. The published evidence says the opposite happened on its sample. Regulated venues showed the round-size clustering that human traders produce. Many unregulated venues showed too little of it: their sizes looked typed by a program that never picks a round number.

Real world

Cong, Li, Tang and Yang, "Crypto Wash Trading", NBER working paper w30783, read at source; the published version appeared in Management Science (reported). On 29 venues from 9 July to 3 November 2019, 3 regulated and 26 unregulated split by web traffic into 10 Tier-1 and 16 Tier-2, they report that round trades make up "around 30%" of trades on the regulated venues, while unregulated Tier-1 and Tier-2 venues "contain little clustering": 30 and 50 percent of those venues, respectively, showed no trade-size clustering in any pair they examined. On their 2019 sample, the anomaly was too little roundness, not too much.

Read the direction carefully, because it decides how the test has to be built. A screen that only fires when roundness is too high would have passed every venue in that finding. Part 2 builds the band with two sides for exactly this reason, and the engine's self test checks it on every page load.

Self-studyFrom a share to an estimate, and why the estimate is contested4 min▶

The study did not stop at a screen. It estimated, on the regulated venues, how much unrounded volume normally comes with a given amount of round volume, then treated any unrounded volume on an unregulated venue above that ratio as wash. The authors' estimate on their 2019 sample is an average of 77.5 percent of unregulated venues' volume, median 79.1 percent. They state the bounds themselves: their first-digit estimates are "significantly lower"; if genuine algorithmic trading inflates unrounded volume, the roundness estimates "should be viewed more as upper bounds"; and venues may have adapted since.

An asset manager's presentation to the US securities regulator in March 2019, reported rather than read at source, put the figure at about 95 percent of one aggregator's reported volume for one asset, across 81 venues over four days, using trade-size histograms, volume patterns and spread behaviour. The two numbers do not contradict each other and do not agree either. Different denominators: unregulated venues' own volume against one aggregator's total. Different samples: 26 venues against 81. Different windows: about four months against four days. Different methods: a regression benchmarked on regulated venues against an industry review. Neither is "the" wash rate, and a page that quotes one without the other has picked a side it did not argue for.

Part 2 · covers clustering and the two-sided band

Clustering: the five most common sizes, and a band with two sides

13 min live

The second test asks a related question: how much of the tape sits on its five favourite sizes? Count every size, take the five most frequent, add their counts and divide by all trades. On Tape A the five are 10 units (85 trades), 2 (73), 20 (71), 3 (64) and 1 (52), together 11.5 percent of 3,000 trades, out of 989 distinct sizes. A crowd's favourites are round and modest. A bot's favourites are either a handful of lots it repeats endlessly, or nothing in particular.

LiveCalibrate both readings on venues you trust4 min▶

A round share of 29.0 percent is neither high nor low until honest venues say what normal is. Forty honest constructed venues, never used for anything else, read a mean round share of 29.96 percent with a standard deviation of 0.84 points, and a mean cluster share of 12.17 percent with a standard deviation of 0.67. At k = 2, two honest standard deviations either side of the mean, the band is 28.3 to 31.6 percent round and 10.8 to 13.5 percent clustered. The widget measures this once when the page loads and judges every tape against it.

Widen k and the band widens on both sides: 27.9 to 32.0 round and 10.5 to 13.8 clustered at k = 2.5, 27.5 to 32.5 and 10.2 to 14.2 at k = 3, 26.6 to 33.3 and 9.5 to 14.8 at k = 4. A wider band fires on fewer honest venues and catches a bot later. Session 7 measures both costs on 200 fresh honest venues; tonight the default is k = 2.

Tape A reads 29.0 and 11.5, its partner venue 29.4 and 12.0, all four inside. Nothing in either honest tape was tuned to land there; the band was measured on other venues.

LiveWhy the band has two sides5 min▶

Plant the round-lot bot on the partner venue at 10 percent of its reported volume. Its round share jumps to 44.9 percent and its cluster share to 28.2, both far above the band, because every wash size is one of seven lots and the lots pile up on the marks the crowd already favours. At 70 percent the readings are 90.1 and 79.0. This is the direction everyone expects.

Now switch to the flat bot at 10 percent. Round share falls to 21.7 percent and cluster share to 8.8, both below the band. The flat bot's four-digit sizes almost never land on a round mark, so it dilutes the crowd's round trades, and it spreads its trades over so many distinct sizes that no new favourites form. At 70 percent the readings are 3.6 and 1.4. This is the direction the published study found on unregulated venues.

A test written as "fire when the share is too high" passes the flat bot at every share. That is not a hypothetical bug; it is the first version most people write, and it is precisely the fault the engine's fifth self-test property exists to catch: a bot with too few round sizes must still fire the roundness test. The fix is small: fire when the reading is outside the band on either side, by absolute distance from the honest mean.

The roundness and clustering tests are two of the three the bench combines; the first-digit test from session 4 is the third, and it is one-sided, because more deviation from the law is the only anomaly it reads. A venue is flagged when at least two of the three fire. Session 7 measures what that rule costs on honest venues.

LiveThe copy bot is the honest limit, not a failure4 min▶

Switch to the copy bot. At 70 percent of the partner venue's reported volume it reads 30.7 percent round and 12.6 percent clustered, inside both bands. The copy bot samples the honest size distribution, so it types like the crowd: as round as the crowd, as clustered as the crowd. A test that reads only the size column has nothing to read.

That is a statement about the size column, not about the tests. Size-based detectors catch bots that leave a size habit, and a careful bot can choose not to leave one. What the copy bot cannot hide is the pair structure every wash trade on the bench has: a buy and a sell of the same size, seconds apart, on opposite sides. None of tonight's tests reads that. Session 6 plants the pairs and session 7 measures this limit, so that "inside the band" is never written as "honest".

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

One camp reads too little roundness as a strong signal of wash. Their case is the published finding: the regulated venues in the sample showed the crowd's round-size clustering, many unregulated venues did not, and a venue whose sizes look typed by nobody is a venue whose volume deserves questions.

The other camp points to the authors' own caveat. Genuine algorithmic traders also produce unrounded sizes, splitting orders and sizing positions by formula, so a venue full of legitimate algorithms would read low on roundness for honest reasons. On that view a low round share is a prompt to ask who the venue's traders are, not a measurement of wash, and any estimate built on it is closer to an upper bound, which is how the authors describe it.

Both camps keep the test two-sided. The disagreement is about how much a reading below the floor proves on its own, and it is one reason the bench never flags a venue on one test alone. The general family of detectors for unusual values, z-scores, isolation forests and control charts, lives in learn-anomaly-detection-with-phoebe; tonight's two tests are forensic ones specific to a self-reported tape.

Part 3 · build-along

Build the round flag and the cluster share, then judge on both sides

15 min hands on

Six steps with pandas and numpy on any tape you have as a CSV with sizes stored as integer micro-units, as Harrowgate stores them since session 1. The comments quote what the constructed Tape A and the bench give; your numbers will differ on any other tape, which is the point. Run the code in a scratch folder, never where the outputs could land in a repository.

Load the size column as integers

The round flag is exact only on integers. If your feed sends floats, convert once at a stated precision, as in session 1, before any test runs.

python · integer sizesimport numpy as np, pandas as pd tape = pd.read_csv("tape.csv") # columns: t, side, size_u, px sizes = tape["size_u"] # integer micro-units: 10 units is 10000000 assert sizes.dtype.kind == "i" and (sizes > 0).all() print(len(sizes), "trades,", sizes.nunique(), "distinct sizes") # Tape A: 3000 trades, 989 distinct

The round flag: strip trailing zeros, one digit left

Integer arithmetic only. No float division, no tolerance.

python · round flag, exactdef is_round(m: int) -> bool: while m > 0 and m % 10 == 0: m //= 10 # 10000000 -> 1, 2500000 -> 25 return m < 10 # one significant digit left round_flag = sizes.map(is_round) print(round(round_flag.mean() * 100, 1), "percent round") # Tape A: 29.0

The five most common sizes and their share

Break ties the same way every time, so two runs on the same tape never pick different favourites. The engine breaks a tie by the smaller size.

python · top five, cluster sharevc = sizes.value_counts() vc = vc.sort_index().sort_values(ascending=False, kind="stable") # ties: smaller size first top5 = vc.head(5) print([(s / 1_000_000, int(n)) for s, n in top5.items()]) # Tape A: [(10.0, 85), (2.0, 73), (20.0, 71), (3.0, 64), (1.0, 52)] print(round(top5.sum() / len(sizes) * 100, 1), "percent in the top five") # Tape A: 11.5

Calibrate both readings on honest tapes, before you look at the suspect

Same function for the honest tapes and the suspect, same tape length, honest tapes chosen for a reason you write down. Population standard deviation, as the bench uses.

python · the honest banddef readings(sizes: pd.Series) -> dict: vc = sizes.value_counts().sort_index().sort_values(ascending=False, kind="stable") return {"round": sizes.map(is_round).mean(), "cluster": vc.head(5).sum() / len(sizes)} honest = pd.DataFrame([readings(pd.read_csv(f)["size_u"]) for f in honest_files]) mu, sd = honest.mean(), honest.std(ddof=0) print((mu * 100).round(2).to_dict(), (sd * 100).round(2).to_dict()) # constructed bench, 40 honest venues: round 29.96 sd 0.84, cluster 12.17 sd 0.67 k = 2 print(((mu - k * sd) * 100).round(1).to_dict(), ((mu + k * sd) * 100).round(1).to_dict()) # k = 2: round 28.3 to 31.6, cluster 10.8 to 13.5

Two-sided z-scores, and say which side

Fire on the absolute distance from the honest mean. Then report the side, because above and below mean different bots.

python · two sidess = pd.Series(readings(pd.read_csv("suspect.csv")["size_u"])) z = (s - mu) / sd fires = z.abs() > k # two-sided: never z > k alone side = np.where(z > k, "above", np.where(z < -k, "below", "inside")) print(pd.DataFrame({"pct": (s * 100).round(1), "z": z.round(1), "fires": fires, "side": side})) # partner venue, flat bot at 10 percent: round 21.7 and cluster 8.8, both below

Prove the one-sided version is wrong on your own data

Run the naive test beside the correct one on a planted flat bot (session 6 shows how to plant one). If the two agree, your planted bot is not flat enough, not the test right.

python · the bug, on purposeone_sided = z > k # the first version most people write print("one-sided fires:", one_sided.to_dict()) print("two-sided fires:", fires.to_dict()) # a flat bot: one-sided all False, two-sided all True. The floor is half the test.

The habit to keep. Count round sizes with integers. Break ties in the top five the same way every time. Calibrate on honest venues before you look at the suspect one. Judge on both sides of the band and print which side. And never write "inside the band, therefore honest": a copy bot at 70 percent sits inside both.

Check yourself

Three questions

Why must the roundness and clustering tests fire on both sides of the honest band?

On the partner venue at 10 percent wash, the round-lot bot reads 44.9 and 28.2, above; the flat bot reads 21.7 and 8.8, below. A test that only watches the ceiling passes the flat bot at every share. Two sides cost something too: at the same k a two-sided test has two edges to cross, so it fires on honest venues at least as often as a one-sided one.

What did Cong, Li, Tang and Yang report about roundness on unregulated venues in their 2019 sample?

Read at source: unregulated Tier-1 and Tier-2 venues "contain little clustering", with 30 and 50 percent of them showing no trade-size clustering in any pair. The anomaly on their sample was too little roundness, which is why the test here has a floor as well as a ceiling.

At 70 percent wash the copy bot reads 30.7 percent round and 12.6 percent clustered, inside both bands. Why does it pass?

The copy bot is the honest limit of every size-based detector: it leaves no size habit by construction. 70 percent is not small, and the band was measured on honest venues only. What remains visible is the pair structure, same size, opposite side, seconds apart, which session 6 plants and these tests do not read.

Before session 6

Homework

About forty minutes, on any size or amount column your team owns.

Round share and top five on a column you know

Trade sizes, order quantities, payment amounts. Run steps 2 and 3, then write one sentence on why the top five are what they are: a price list, a default in a form, a habit of the people who type them. If the answer is "a default in a form", the column measures the form, not the people.

Write the side down before you compute it

Pick a second source you trust less. Predict whether it will read above, inside or below the first one's band, and why, then run steps 4 and 5. Session 6 plants all three bot styles on purpose; bring your prediction and whether it held.
