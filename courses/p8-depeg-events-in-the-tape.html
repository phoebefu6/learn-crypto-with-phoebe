Depeg events in the tape

Every print on the tape is quoted in a unit, and on this kind of feed that unit is itself a traded thing designed to sit at 1.000 against a reference. Restating a day's tape into the reference is therefore not a conversion factor applied at the end. It is a join between two time series that keep their own clocks: the prints, and the unit's own level. Tonight you build that join the way a data engineer has to, as-of and backward with a tolerance, in integers, on Harrowgate's constructed tape. Then you watch a constructed depeg do something the quoted tape cannot show: at a 6 percent depth the quoted tape never moves more than 0.046 percent in an hour, and the restated tape falls 3.96 percent in one. Nothing here is a real unit, a real asset or a price of anything.

Part 0

Harrowgate has to restate yesterday, and the unit slipped

Harrowgate is a constructed data team at a fund-administration firm. Its daily reference level is published in a reference unit, while every venue it reads quotes in a different unit, one designed to track the reference at 1.000. Yesterday that unit slipped for most of the day. The request on the team's desk is short: restate the whole day's tape into the reference unit, show the hour it moved, and make sure the same answer can be produced again next year if an auditor asks. Leader session 4 explains why the restated number is the one the reader needs. Tonight is how it is built, and what has to be kept so it can be built again.

LiveWhy a depeg is a storage problem first3 min▶

A depeg looks like a market event and arrives as a data event. The venue's tape keeps printing in the quoting unit, at prices that barely move, because the asset did not move against the unit. What moved was the unit, and the unit's level is not on the tape at all. It comes from a second source, on its own clock, at its own grain. Every restated number is therefore the product of two observations made at two different moments, and the question "which unit level did this print use?" has an answer only if somebody stored it.

That is why the engineering here is mostly about time and storage rather than arithmetic. The multiplication is one line. Deciding which level each print meets, what happens when the right level has not arrived, and what has to be kept so the multiplication can be redone with a corrected level, is the job.

Real world

Harrowgate's first restatement used one figure for the whole day, the unit's daily average, because that was the number its reference feed published. On the constructed depeg, a 10:00 print and a 16:00 print were put on the same ruler when the ruler had moved between them by several percent. The restated day was smooth, plausible and wrong in every hour that mattered. Harrowgate is constructed; restating at the wrong grain is a common way to get a calm-looking wrong answer.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Quoting unit. What the venue's prices are expressed in. Here a traded thing designed to sit at 1.000 against the reference.
  • Reference unit. What the report is stated in, and what the reader settles in.
  • Unit level. The quoting unit's value in the reference, as an integer in millionths. One level per minute on the constructed path, stamped at the start of the minute.
  • Restate. Multiply a quoted price by the unit level that applied at that print's moment, and round in a stated way.
  • As-of join. Match each row of one series to the latest row of another at or before it. Also called a backward or point-in-time join.
  • Tolerance. How old a matched unit level may be before it is refused. A decision, written down, set from the unit series' own grain.
  • Band. How far from 1.000 the unit may sit before the report says so. Wider than the unit's ordinary jitter, which is a thousandth on the constructed path.
  • Depth. How far the constructed unit falls during the declared depeg: 0, 3, 6 or 12 percent in the widget.
Part 1 · covers the restatement rule and the as-of join

Restating a tape is a time join

14 min live

Every print is quoted in the unit. The restated price is the quoted price times the unit's level at that print's moment. That sentence hides two decisions: what "at that moment" means when the unit series has its own clock, and how the product is rounded back into ticks. Get either wrong and the restated tape is still a tidy column of numbers, which is what makes this kind of error survive review.

LiveThe rule, in integers5 min▶

The engine running in this page holds every quoted price as an integer number of ticks (100,009 ticks reads as 100.009) and every unit level as an integer number of millionths of the reference (a level of exactly 1.000 is 1,000,000). The restated price is computed as floor(px × level / 1,000,000 + 0.5): multiply, divide back into ticks, round half up. In integer-only form that is (px × level + 500,000) // 1,000,000, and the two agree for every positive price.

Three reasons to insist on this form. A rerun next year gives the same tick, on any machine, in any language, because nothing passed through a float. The rounding rule is stated, so a restated tick that differs by one from someone else's has an explanation rather than a mystery. And the product fits comfortably in a 64-bit integer: a price around 100,000 ticks times a level around 1,000,000 is around 1011.

Which level is "the" level for a print? On the constructed path the unit has one level per minute, stamped at the start of the minute, and a print at second t uses the level of minute floor(t / 60). That is an as-of join written as integer division: the latest stamp at or before the print. It works here because the constructed unit series has a level for every minute. A real unit feed will not be that tidy, and the general form of the join has to say what happens when it is not.

LiveWhy as-of, and why backward5 min▶

An exact-time join returns almost nothing. The unit series ticks on its own clock, at minute starts; the prints arrive on the venue's clock, at whatever second a trade happened. The 12:00:39 print has no unit level stamped 12:00:39. Joined on equal timestamps, almost every print on the tape would get a null, and the job would either fail loudly or, worse, drop the unmatched rows and restate the handful left.

A nearest-time join reads the future. Matching each print to the closest unit stamp in either direction sends a 12:00:39 print to the 12:01 level, a value that did not exist when the trade happened. In a calm hour the difference is a thousandth. On a depeg hour, when the unit is falling every minute, it systematically restates the morning with the afternoon's ruler. A backtest built that way has looked ahead, and nothing in the output says so.

Backward as-of is the only one of the three that uses information available at the print. Each print takes the latest unit level at or before its own time, and only if that level is younger than a tolerance. The direction is not a style choice; it is the statement that the restatement used what was known at the moment the trade happened.

Self-studyWhen the unit feed goes quiet4 min▶

Figure A draws the 12:01 level as missing. The engine's path has every minute, so this is the drawing of a fault, not of the constructed run. With the level missing, a plain backward join quietly reaches past 12:01 to 12:00 and restates the 12:01 print with a level from the minute before. On a calm day nobody would ever see it. On a depeg morning a stale level is exactly wrong in the direction the unit is moving.

A tolerance turns that silence into a decision. Set it from the unit series' own grain: on a series stamped every minute, a print can be at most 59 seconds after the latest stamp, so anything older than about a minute means a level is missing, not late. Rows past the tolerance get a null restated price and a stale flag. Some teams then fill them deliberately with the older level and a second flag that says so; others leave them null. Either is defensible when it is written down. Filling silently is not.

Real world

One public index rulebook, read at source and left unnamed, faces the same question for its own inputs: a partition whose data could not be retrieved in time is excluded and the remaining weights are adjusted, and when there are no trades in the whole window, "the price is not published". It does not carry an old number forward and present it as current. The restatement job inherits the same professional norm for the same reason.

What counts as "late" versus "missing" on a live feed, and how a job decides that a slice has fully arrived, is a watermark question. The stream mechanics are taught in learn-streaming-data-with-phoebe; here the point is the policy.

Part 2 · covers the depeg hour and the storage schema

The hour the quoted tape hides

13 min live

The constructed unit path is declared, not discovered. On every tape it sits at 1.000 give or take a thousandth of jitter. From minute 600, 10:00, it falls linearly over 90 minutes to its depth, holds there for 120 minutes, and recovers linearly over 240, back at 1.000 by 17:30. The widget restates the whole day through the rule above, then reads the last print of every hour in both units. Nothing is smoothed and no print is dropped. The quoted column and the restated column are the same 3,000 prints.

LiveRead the hourly table at 6 percent4 min▶

Start on Tape A at a 6 percent depth. Run your eye down the "last quoted" column first. It wanders around 100.000 all day, and the summary line under the table names its largest hour-over-hour move: -0.046 percent, at hour 4, six hours before anything happens to the unit. That is ordinary trading noise on the constructed tape.

Now the "restated move" column. Flat until 09:00, then the shaded rows begin. The largest move of the day is -3.96 percent, at hour 10, the hour the unit is falling, and the unit bottoms at 0.939016. The recovery hours are visible too, as a run of positive moves after the hold. None of that is in the quoted column, because none of it happened to the asset against the unit.

Then find the first print at or after noon, which the summary line prints in full. It lands at 12:00:39, quoted at 100.009, and restates to 94.025 reference units. That single row is Figure A's worked case, now computed rather than drawn: the print, the unit level of its own minute, and the integer product.

LiveMove the depth, then set it to zero4 min▶

Switch the depth to 3 and then to 12 percent. The unit bottoms at 0.969016 and 0.879016, and the restated tape's worst hour becomes -1.99 percent and -7.89 percent, both at hour 10. The quoted summary does not change at all: -0.046 percent at hour 4 at every depth. The quoted tape never moves more than 0.046 percent in an hour at any depth, because the move is in the unit. A monitor on the quoted tape is not broken on a depeg day; it is measuring correctly against a ruler that is moving with it.

Now set the depth to 0. There is no depeg, the unit never leaves its thousandth and bottoms at 0.999000, and still the restated tape's worst hour is -0.155 percent, at hour 21, against -0.046 quoted. The noon print restates to 100.025 rather than 100.009, purely from the jitter. Restating adds the unit's own noise to every number, so a band and any alert threshold on the restated tape have to sit wider than that noise, or they fire on an ordinary evening.

Switch tapes and the quoted figures change, because every constructed tape has its own noise. The shape does not: the quoted summary is identical at every depth on the same tape, and the restated summary follows the depth into hour 10.

LiveWhat to store so a restatement can be redone3 min▶

The prints, as quoted. Append only, at print grain, with the quoting unit named on every row. The restatement must never write back into this table. If a test can detect a single changed quoted tick after a restatement run, the pipeline has a bug that will one day destroy the only observation.

The unit levels, as observed. One row per unit per stamp, as integers, with where the level came from and how it was reconciled. The unit has its own venues and its own many prices, so its level is a reconciled number exactly like the asset's in session 3, and it can be corrected. A correction is a new row with a later receipt time, never an overwrite.

The restated view, with its as-of key. For every print: the unit stamp it actually used, the level, the age of that level, the restated tick, the out-of-band flag and the rule version. The level_ts_used column is what makes the view auditable: an auditor can see exactly which ruler each print met, and a rerun with a corrected level can be diffed row by row against the original. The flag marks every row where the unit sat outside the band the team wrote down, wider than its ordinary jitter, so a reader of any downstream number can tell a depeg-hour row from an ordinary one.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree3 min▶

One camp materialises the restated view as a table, partitioned by day, because downstream jobs read it constantly and recomputing a join over every print on every read is wasteful. On this view the rule version and the unit stamp used are stored per row, and a correction to the unit's level triggers a rebuild of the affected days, recorded as a new version.

The other camp keeps restatement as a view that is computed at read time and never stored, on the grounds that a stored derived table is a second source of truth that will drift from its inputs the first time someone forgets a rebuild. On this view the only stored things are observations, and the cost of recomputing is the price of never being out of date.

Both camps keep the two observed tables forever and both can name the unit stamp each print used. The disagreement is about where the derived rows live, and it matters most when a unit level is corrected after a report has gone out: the first camp has to find and rebuild, the second has to explain why last month's report no longer reproduces from today's view.

Part 3 · build-along

Build the restatement, then prove it touched nothing it should not

15 min hands on

Six steps with pandas, on a tape as a CSV with columns t in seconds, side, size and px in integer ticks, plus the quoting unit's level as a second CSV with a minute-start time and the level as an integer in millionths. On the constructed Tape A at a 6 percent depth the widget's answers are a worst quoted hour of -0.046 percent at hour 4 and a worst restated hour of -3.96 percent at hour 10. Off the default seed, and on any tape of your own, your numbers will differ; the rule and the tests do not. Run the code in a scratch folder, never where the outputs could land in a repository.

Load both series on their own clocks

Two files, two clocks. Convert both to timestamps, sort both, and keep a copy of the quoted prices to test against at the end.

python · two seriesimport pandas as pd MICRO = 1_000_000 prints = pd.read_csv("tape.csv") # t (seconds), side, size, px (integer ticks, quoted) units = pd.read_csv("unit_levels.csv") # minute_start (seconds), level_micro (integer) prints["ts"] = pd.to_datetime(prints["t"], unit="s") units["level_ts"] = pd.to_datetime(units["minute_start"], unit="s") prints = prints.sort_values("ts", kind="stable").reset_index(drop=True) units = units.sort_values("level_ts").reset_index(drop=True) assert units["level_ts"].is_unique # one level per stamp, or the join is ambiguous quoted_before = prints["px"].copy() # the observation, kept for the test in step 6

Join as-of, backward, with a tolerance

Each print takes the latest unit level at or before it. The tolerance comes from the unit series' grain: stamped every minute, so a level older than that means one is missing. With several quoting units, add by="unit" so a print never meets another unit's level.

python · merge_asofTOL = pd.Timedelta("60s") # the unit series stamps every minute j = pd.merge_asof(prints, units[["level_ts", "level_micro"]], left_on="ts", right_on="level_ts", direction="backward", tolerance=TOL) j["level_age_s"] = (j["ts"] - j["level_ts"]).dt.total_seconds() j["unit_stale"] = j["level_micro"].isna() # past the tolerance: no level, said so print(j["unit_stale"].sum(), "prints with no unit level inside the tolerance") print(j["level_age_s"].max(), "seconds, the oldest level any print used")

Restate in integers, nulls where the join refused

Nullable integers carry the refusal through the arithmetic: a stale row stays null instead of becoming a number that looks observed. The rounding is half up, stated once.

python · integer restatementlvl = j["level_micro"].astype("Int64") j["px_ref"] = (j["px"].astype("Int64") * lvl + MICRO // 2) // MICRO noon = j[j["t"] >= 43200].iloc[0] print(noon["ts"], noon["px"], noon["level_micro"], noon["px_ref"]) # the first print at or after noon

Flag the unit when it leaves its band

The band is a written-down decision, set wider than the unit's ordinary jitter of a thousandth. Put the value in the rule version so every row says which band judged it.

python · out-of-band flagJITTER = MICRO // 1000 # the constructed unit's ordinary noise BAND = 5 * JITTER # your decision; write it down, do not discover it j["unit_out_of_band"] = (lvl - MICRO).abs().gt(BAND).fillna(False) j["rule_version"] = f"asof-backward-{int(TOL.total_seconds())}s|band-{BAND}|half-up" print(j.loc[j["unit_out_of_band"], "ts"].agg(["min", "max"])) # when the unit was out

Hourly last print, in both units

Take the last print row of each hour, then compute the hour-over-hour move in each unit. Avoid resample().last() here: it skips nulls column by column, so a stale last print would silently be replaced by an earlier one in the restated column only.

python · hourly moveshour = j["ts"].dt.floor("h") last = j.groupby(hour).tail(1).set_index(hour[j.groupby(hour).tail(1).index]) moves = last[["px", "px_ref"]].astype("float64").pct_change() * 100 moves["band"] = last["unit_out_of_band"] print(moves.round(3)) print("worst quoted hour: ", moves["px"].abs().idxmax(), round(moves["px"].loc[moves["px"].abs().idxmax()], 3)) print("worst restated hour:", moves["px_ref"].abs().idxmax(), round(moves["px_ref"].loc[moves["px_ref"].abs().idxmax()], 3))

Test that the quoted series is unchanged, and that the view rebuilds

Two assertions to keep in the job, not in a notebook. The first proves the restatement never wrote into the observation and never dropped or duplicated a print. The second proves the stored columns are enough to redo it.

python · the testsassert len(j) == len(prints) # merge_asof keeps every left row, exactly once pd.testing.assert_series_equal(j["px"], quoted_before, check_names=False) stored = j[["trade_id", "px", "level_ts", "level_micro", "px_ref", "rule_version"]] if "trade_id" in j else j redo = (stored["px"].astype("Int64") * stored["level_micro"].astype("Int64") + MICRO // 2) // MICRO pd.testing.assert_series_equal(redo, stored["px_ref"], check_names=False) print("quoted unchanged, restatement reproducible from what is stored")

The habit to keep. When a number is restated, ask which unit level each row met and when that level was stamped. When a join is on time, ask which direction it looks and how far. When a derived table is stored, ask whether the observed tables beside it are enough to rebuild it, and whether a test proves the observation was not touched.

Check yourself

Three questions

On the constructed 6 percent depeg, the quoted tape's worst hour is -0.046 percent while the restated tape's is -3.96 percent. Why does the quoted tape look calm?

All 3,000 prints are still there and nothing is smoothed; the quoted and restated columns are the same prints. The quoted tape's -0.046 percent at hour 4 is ordinary noise from before the depeg and is identical at every depth. The move lives in the unit, which is a second series that is not on the tape.

Why does the restatement join prints to unit levels as-of and backward, rather than on exactly equal timestamps?

The 12:00:39 print has no unit level stamped 12:00:39; an exact join would leave almost every print unmatched. A nearest join would send it to the 12:01 level, a value that did not exist when it traded, which on a falling unit restates the morning with the afternoon's ruler. Backward as-of with a tolerance uses what was known, and says so when nothing recent enough was.

An auditor asks Harrowgate to redo last year's depeg-day restatement with a corrected unit feed. What must have been stored for that to be possible?

Restated prices alone cannot be rechecked or rebuilt. A daily average puts a 10:00 print and a 16:00 print on the same ruler when the ruler moved between them. Keeping both observations, plus the as-of key and the rule version per row, lets a rerun with the corrected levels be diffed row by row against what was published.

Homework

About forty minutes, on a join your own team already runs.

Find one time join and name its direction

Any job that attaches a rate, a price, a status or a mapping to events by time. Write down whether it joins exactly, nearest or backward, what tolerance it uses, and what it does when nothing is recent enough. If the answer is "it fills from the last known value", find out whether anything records that it did.

Check that the key it used was kept

For one derived number, try to name the exact row of the second series it was computed from. If you cannot, the derivation cannot be redone after a correction.
