Why the market-data playbook breaks here

A data team that has run a conventional market-data pipeline for years owns a playbook that works: take the consolidated tape, trust the audited volume, join on the account, cut at the close, and wait for the correction when a print is wrong. Every step leans on something the market arrangement provides without being asked. Sessions 1 to 4 each took one of those away. Tonight puts them side by side as mechanisms, adds the two that hurt most, identity and ground truth, and ends with the checklist a leader runs on any crypto-market figure before it goes on a slide. Nothing here is investment advice, and nothing here is a real venue, asset or price.

Part 0

Harrowgate, the week the old playbook stopped working

Harrowgate is a constructed data team at a fund-administration firm. By now it writes the boundary beside every daily number, screens its venues, names its index method and stores the quoting unit's own level. Each of those fixes was right. Then a senior colleague asks a reasonable question, "which venue matters most, how many traders does it have, and how sure are we", and the team discovers that none of its fixes answers it. The playbook did not have four holes. It had one assumption, that somebody else guarantees the record, and that assumption is gone everywhere at once.

LiveThe playbook, written down for once3 min▶

Ask a market-data team what it does and you hear tools and tables. Ask what it assumes and you get five short sentences that nobody writes down because they have always been true. The record comes from one place that someone else audits. Every trade belongs to an account, and every day ends at a close. The unit prices are quoted in holds still. And when a print is wrong, an official correction arrives and the team can check itself against it.

Sessions 1 to 4 took away the close, the audited volume, the single price and the still unit, one at a time. Tonight is about why fixing them one at a time is not enough: every workaround for one of them quietly assumes another still holds. The two that remain, identity and the correction, are the ones with no engineering fix at all, only a change in what a leader is willing to sign.

Real world

One public index rulebook, read at source, does not trust the raw record either. Before any venue's trades count toward its fixing, the venue is screened quarterly on criteria including "Regulated by an independent government body", "KYC/AML controls" and "Trading Policies", plus five years of operation and a liquidity floor of 0.5 percent of observed liquidity in the pair over three months. That is what a mature practitioner does when nobody guarantees the source: decide who is allowed into the record before computing anything on it.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Consolidated tape. One record of every trade across the venues trading an instrument, assembled by an operator. A conventional feed has one; a crypto feed does not. Session 3.
  • Reported volume. The number a venue publishes about itself. A claim, not a measurement. Session 2.
  • Wash trade. A buy and a sell of the same size that leave the trader's position unchanged and add volume to the record. Practitioner session 6 plants them.
  • Identity. Which account, firm or person stands behind a trade. On a conventional record it is there for whoever is entitled to see it; on a crypto venue's public tape it is absent.
  • Ground truth. The correct answer, published by someone with the authority to publish it, that a team can score its own numbers against.
  • Correction. An official fix to a wrong print, after which the record is republished. The mechanism that lets a team learn from its errors.
  • Feedback loop. Any path by which an output changes its own input. Here, a league table that rewards a venue for the volume it reports.
  • Due diligence. The questions a leader asks before a number is allowed to carry a decision. Tonight's build-along.
Part 1 · covers the source and its feedback loop

One audited tape against many venues that report themselves

12 min live

The first move of the market-data playbook is so automatic that it does not feel like a move: take the volume from the feed. On a conventional feed that is safe, because the volume was counted by an operator with an auditor behind it, from one consolidated record. On a crypto feed every venue counts its own volume and publishes the count, and the count decides where the venue sits in the tables that send it business. The source is not only unaudited. It is paid to be larger.

LiveThe source is a claim, and the claim is paid4 min▶

On a conventional feed the question "where does the volume come from" has a dull answer: the operator counted it from the consolidated record and somebody audits the operator. On a crypto feed the answer is "the venue says so". There is no consolidated tape to count from and no auditor behind the count. A data team that takes the number from the feed is taking a statement from the party with the most interest in it being large.

The loop is what turns an unaudited source into an inflated one. Tables of venues ranked by reported volume send traders and attention to the top of the table. A venue that reports more climbs; a venue that climbs gets flow. In their 2019 sample, Cong, Li, Tang and Yang estimate that 70 percent wash in a venue's reported volume moved it up 46 positions on a third-party ranking. That is their estimate, on their sample, and the mechanism is the point rather than the size: the output of the ranking rewards the input to the ranking.

The playbook move that breaks is weighting by volume. It is standard practice and correct when the volume is real. The rulebooks did not abandon it; they screen which venues may contribute before any weight is computed. The bench below shows why that order matters.

LiveRead the bench at 70 percent, then change one setting4 min▶

On Tape A with the round-lot washer at 70 percent, venue B carries 52.7 percent of the honest volume and 78.8 percent of the reported volume. A slide built from reported volume shows a venue that dominates; the honest market is close to an even split. The three detectors all fire, 3 of 3, because a bot cycling round lots leaves a shape no crowd of human traders leaves: roundness 90.1 percent and the top five sizes covering 79.0 percent of trades, against honest bands of 28.3 to 31.6 and 10.8 to 13.5.

Now switch the washer to "copies the honest sizes" and leave everything else alone. Zero of three fire. Roundness reads 30.7 percent, clustering 12.6, the first-digit deviation 0.0074, all inside the honest bands. The weight moves exactly as far, because the wash share has not changed. That is not a broken detector. A washer that samples the honest size distribution is invisible to every size-based test by construction; what still gives it away is the matched-pair structure, same size, opposite side, seconds apart, and these three tests do not read it.

Try Tape B at 10 percent with the round bot: the washed venue moves from rank 2 to rank 1. A ranking moves before a slide looks strange.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

One camp says the only defensible source is a screened one: decide which venues are allowed into the record, on criteria a stranger can check, and then weight by volume as usual. This is what the public index rulebooks do, and it keeps the conventional arithmetic intact once the gate is passed.

The other camp says screening moves the trust problem rather than solving it, because the criteria are themselves partly self-reported, and a washer that copies honest sizes passes every statistical screen. On this view volume should never be a weight at all on an unaudited feed; use counts of independent observations, or medians that one venue cannot drag.

Both agree on what matters for a leader: the source is a decision, not a given, and the decision has to be written next to the number. Practitioner sessions 4 to 7 build the detectors, and practitioner session 10 runs this bench in full.

Part 2 · covers identity and time

Accounts and a settlement day against no identity and no close

12 min live

The second move of the playbook is the join. A conventional trade record carries an account for whoever is entitled to see it, and it belongs to a trading day that ends at a close and settles on a known day after. Customer questions, concentration questions and daily questions are all joins on those two columns. A crypto venue's public tape has a time, a side, a size and a price. The two columns the join needs are not there.

LiveNo identity means no customer, no concentration, no count4 min▶

Most of the questions a senior colleague asks about a market are questions about people. How many traders are active? How concentrated is the volume? Is one participant on both sides of a lot of trades? On a conventional record those are joins on the account column, sometimes restricted, never impossible. On a crypto venue's public tape there is no account column. Tape A has 3,000 trades in a day. It does not have a number of traders, and no amount of cleaning will produce one.

The wash pair in the figure shows the cost. On venue B's constructed tape at 10 percent wash, a sell and then a buy of 50 units print at t=96 and 97 seconds. A conventional surveillance team would check whether the two sides share an owner. Here the two rows look exactly like two strangers who happened to pick the same size a second apart. The bench knows they are one washer because it planted them; the tape alone does not.

The instinct to reach for the chain is natural and belongs elsewhere: what can and cannot be said about identity from on-chain addresses is the subject of learn-web3-with-phoebe, and linking records that may be the same party is learn-entity-resolution-with-phoebe. Neither turns a venue's tape into an account ledger.

LiveNo close means every daily number carries a choice4 min▶

Session 1 measured this on a constructed five-day tape: the first full-day return reads +0.052, -0.020, -0.061 or +0.054 percent depending on whether the day ends at 00:00, 08:00, 16:00 or 21:00. The playbook's daily join, today's close to yesterday's, has no fixed key. Every daily table, every day-over-day chart and every month-end figure is built on a boundary somebody chose.

Identity and time fail together for a reason. On a conventional market both are supplied by the same arrangement: the operator that stops the market also settles the trades against known accounts. Take the operator out and both columns go at once. That is why a team that fixes the boundary still cannot answer "how many traders", and a team that buys an identity product still has to say which day it means.

Self-studyWhat a leader stops promising4 min▶

The practical consequence is a short list of questions a crypto-market data team cannot answer from venue tapes, and should say so before it is asked: unique participants, participant concentration, whether one party is on both sides, and any daily figure without a stated boundary. Leaders who come from conventional markets tend to promise these by reflex, because on their old feed they were cheap.

The professional move is not to refuse the question but to answer the question the data can answer, and say which one it is. "3,000 trades on the venue yesterday, 00:00 boundary; the tape carries no participant identity, so trader counts are not available" is a finished sentence. A trader count that somebody inferred and did not label is a liability that arrives later.

Part 3 · covers ground truth, then the build-along

A corrected print against a number nobody will ever correct

18 min live and hands on

The last move of the playbook is the one teams notice least: being told when they were wrong. A conventional print that is wrong gets busted or corrected by the operator, the record is republished, and the team can score its own pipeline against the fix. That loop is how a market-data team gets better. On a crypto feed no authority will ever publish the true wash share of a venue, the true volume or the true participant count, so the loop never closes. Six minutes on what that does to the cost of being wrong, then the checklist.

LiveWithout a correction, the cost of being wrong is invisible3 min▶

Two published estimates of how much reported volume is wash exist, and nobody will ever publish the answer they are estimating. Cong, Li, Tang and Yang (read at source) estimate wash at 77.5 percent on average of unregulated venues' volume, median 79.1, on 26 unregulated venues over about four months of 2019. They bound it both ways: their Benford-based estimates are "somewhat lower bounds", and if authentic algorithmic trading inflates unrounded volume, the main estimates "should be viewed more as upper bounds". A 2019 industry presentation by Fusaro and Hougan, reported rather than read, put about 95 percent on one aggregator's figure for one asset, across 81 venues over four days. Different denominators, samples, windows and methods. Neither is "the" wash rate, and no correction will ever say which was closer.

The unit of account adds a second kind of uncorrected error. On session 4's constructed 6 percent depeg the quoted tape's worst hour is -0.046 percent and the restated one is -3.96 percent. Nobody republishes the quoted figure when the unit slips; the team has to restate it or stay wrong.

So the only place a detector's accuracy can be measured is a bench where the truth was planted. On the constructed bench, 3 of 200 fresh honest venues are flagged at k=2, 1.5 percent, and the copy bot is never caught at any share up to 70 percent. Those two numbers are the cost of being wrong in each direction, and they exist only because the bench knows the answer the market never gives.

Self-studyWhy fixing one mechanism at a time fails3 min▶

Each workaround leans on another mechanism that is also gone. Screening venues relies on criteria that are partly self-reported. A wash detector needs ground truth to be calibrated on the market, and there is none, so it is calibrated on constructed honest tapes instead. A trader count needs identity. A daily return needs a boundary, and comparing two venues' daily returns needs both to have used the same one. A restatement needs the unit's own level, at the grain of the prints, from a source that is itself one more claim.

The honest summary for a leader: the arithmetic of the playbook carries over intact. What does not carry over is the guarantee under the inputs. Session 6 turns that into a transfer map of what to keep, what to learn and what to unlearn.

The build-along. No code. Four questions a leader asks of any crypto-market figure on a slide, then three constructed figures run through them out loud in the room. Each figure is built from the constructed tapes in sessions 1 to 5, so every answer can be checked on this page or its siblings.

Question 1 · Whose claim is it?

One venue, several, or a table that ranks them? Reported volume or a count somebody else made? Which venues were screened out before the number was computed, and on what criteria? A figure with no answer here is a venue's statement about itself with a chart around it.

Question 2 · Which day, and which unit?

If the figure is daily, what boundary made it a day? If it is a price or a change, what is it quoted in, and was it restated into a reference unit? Sessions 1 and 4 showed that either choice alone can carry the sign or the size.

Question 3 · Does it need an identity the tape does not have?

Traders, users, participants, concentration, "the same party on both sides". If the figure counts people and the source is a venue's tape, somebody inferred it. Ask how, and ask whether the inference is labelled.

Question 4 · Who would ever tell us it is wrong?

Is there a correction mechanism, a published answer, anything to score against? If not, where was the method measured: on a bench with planted truth, with its false-positive rate and the case it cannot catch stated? A method with no bench and no correction has an unknown error rate.

Figure 1 · "Venue B carries 78.8 percent of the volume"

Question 1 fails first: it is reported volume, unscreened. On the honest volume B carries 52.7 percent. The detectors flag a round-lot washer 3 of 3; switch to a washer that copies honest sizes and 0 of 3 fire with the same weight. The finished slide says "78.8 percent of reported volume, unscreened; honest share unknown on a real feed".

Figure 2 · "The asset's worst hour yesterday: -3.96 percent"

Question 2 fails twice. Yesterday by which boundary? And -3.96 percent is the restated figure on session 4's 6 percent depeg; quoted in the venue's own unit the same hour reads -0.046 percent. Both are true. The slide needs the unit named and the quoted figure beside it, or a reader will believe the asset moved when the ruler did.

Figure 3 · "3,000 active traders on the venue yesterday"

Question 3 fails outright. Tape A carries 3,000 trades in a day, and no account column. Someone turned trades into traders. The finished slide says "3,000 trades, 00:00 boundary; participant counts are not available from the tape". Then Question 4: nobody will ever publish the true count, so any inferred one needs its method on the slide.

The habit to keep. When a crypto-market figure arrives on a slide, run the four questions before you discuss the number. Whose claim, which day and unit, which identity it assumes, who would ever correct it. The arithmetic is almost never the problem. The missing guarantee underneath it almost always is.

Check yourself

Three questions

A slide says venue B carries 78.8 percent of the two-venue market. What is the first question to ask?

The source comes first. On the constructed bench 78.8 percent is B's share of reported volume with a washer at 70 percent; its honest share is 52.7. Weighting by volume is correct only after the venues have been screened, which is what the public index rulebooks do.

With a washer that copies honest trade sizes at 70 percent, none of the three detectors fires. What does that mean?

The copy bot's sizes are drawn from the same distribution as the honest ones, so roundness, clustering and the first-digit test read inside the honest bands by construction. Same size, opposite side, seconds apart is still there; these three tests do not read it.

Why can Harrowgate not measure its wash detector's accuracy on the real venues it watches?

A conventional feed corrects wrong prints and the team can score itself against the fix. Here no correction arrives. The bench is where the false-positive rate, 3 of 200 honest venues at k=2, and the case the method cannot catch, the copy bot, become numbers.
